27% of Agent Add-ons Get Blocked and 52% of MCP Servers Are Abandoned — Trust Is Now the Cheapest Moat a Solo Founder Can Build

Supply is no longer the constraint. Two numbers landed in the same week and they point in opposite directions: the agent-skill ecosystem now has roughly 350,000 published skills, over 10,000 public MCP servers and about 97 million monthly SDK downloads — while independent audits of the public registry found 52% of listed MCP servers abandoned and only 17% meeting a reasonable production bar. AIR, the agent-security startup that came out of stealth on September 1 with $50M in seed funding, says about 27% of the publicly available skills and add-ons it scans get filtered out by its security system.

Read those together and the conclusion is uncomfortable but clarifying: shipping something that works has stopped being valuable. Shipping something an enterprise can audit, trust and keep depending on is where the pricing power moved. That is a job a single person can actually do, and one the frontier labs have no patience for.

Key Insights

The bottleneck in the agent economy has shifted from capability to trustworthiness of invocation. Anyone can write a skill; almost nobody maintains one to a standard a regulated buyer will accept.

The numbers that should change your roadmap:

  • 52% / 17% — abandoned vs. production-grade share of public MCP registry listings
  • ~27% — publicly available skills and add-ons filtered out as untrusted by AIR’s system; it has flagged 17,800 untrusted plugins tied to roughly 6.7 million installs
  • $0.02–0.12/min — current all-in price band for full-stack voice agents, against 7¢+ for other voice AI platforms, $0.13+ for offshore human agents and $0.67+ for US call centers
  • ~52x, unchanged — Cognition’s price-to-sales multiple as its valuation went from $26B to ~$47B (annualized revenue $492M → ~$900M). The orchestration layer got repriced on results, not sentiment
  • 1.08% / 92% — AI referral traffic as a share of total sessions / ChatGPT’s share of trackable AI referrals. Meanwhile homepage landings rose from ~25% to nearly 60% of ChatGPT referrals

Market Trends

Capital is now paying for the right to vet

AIR emerged from stealth on September 1 with $50M across two seed rounds — $10M led by Sequoia, then $40M led by Greenoaks, closing within weeks of each other. It does not sell model capability. It sells an enforcement layer that sits between an agent and whatever skill, MCP server or add-on it wants to load: discover which agents are running inside the org, continuously re-verify what those agents have installed, and intercept loads that fall outside a maintained whitelist.

The founders’ analogy is the clearest framing of the category: in the early 2000s you could install a driver without a digital signature. Today you cannot, because a driver loads code into the kernel. Skills and MCP servers do structurally the same thing and have none of that machinery.

The customer profile matters more than the round size. AIR reports 20+ customers, roughly a quarter of them large enterprises, with the strongest pull from financial services and pharmaceuticals — industries where software access and data governance are procurement gates, not features. The category already has company: Zenity raised a $125M Series C in August 2026; Noma Security previously raised $100M.

And the supply-side quality distribution is exactly why the business exists. Over half of public registry servers are unmaintained; only about one in six clears a production bar. On the other side of the ledger, SkillsMP alone lists over 351,000 skills, Vercel’s skills.sh carries 83,000 skills and 8 million installs across 18 agents, and ClawHub holds ~3,200 curated skills with 1.5 million downloads. npm took a decade to reach 350,000 packages. The skills ecosystem did it in two months, with no quality control attached.

What this means for you: stop trying to publish skill number 351,001. Two real openings exist. First, productize trust itself — scanning, continuous re-verification, version contracts, exportable audit reports — because the buyer already exists and the budget sits in regulated industries. Second, go mine the 52%: adopt an abandoned-but-still-installed MCP server, add auth, rate limiting and observability, and sell it as a hosted remote version. The second path is better for a beginner because you inherit existing invocation habit instead of buying new traffic.

Voice agents dropped below human cost, so service businesses became a one-person job

ThunderPhone launched on Product Hunt on September 1 at 2¢/min including models, with tiers at roughly 2¢, 5–6¢ and 9–12¢, plus 1–3¢ surcharges for premium voices and long prompts. Its own comparison set is the useful part:

Option Cost per minute
ThunderPhone (cheapest tier, models included) $0.02
ThunderPhone (highest-intelligence tier) $0.09–0.12
Other voice AI platforms (Vapi / Retell / Bland) $0.07+
Offshore human call center $0.13+
US human call center $0.67+

It also published its 99.4% Big Bench Audio result — eval set and transcripts — on Hugging Face. That matters more than the score: voice-agent reliability is becoming third-party checkable rather than vendor-asserted.

What this changes is not “build a voice toy.” It is the viability of taking over a service business whose core workflow is the phone. That used to be impossible solo because seat cost and scheduling were hard limits. Now the cost line is a few cents a minute plus a SIP trunk, and the real barriers moved to three places: hearing correctly (names, addresses, part numbers, spellings), writing results back into the customer’s CRM or ticketing system, and compliance. All three are solvable by “someone who knows the industry plus off-the-shelf tooling.” None require a research team.

The orchestration layer got repriced on results, which means the copy window closed

Cognition is raising roughly $1B at about a $47B valuation, up ~80% from $26B three months earlier. The detail that matters: annualized revenue went from $492M to roughly $900M over the same window, so the P/S multiple barely moved at ~52x. This was a results repricing, not a multiple expansion. Devin has absorbed the acquired Windsurf as Devin Desktop, turning a single coding agent into an agent umbrella.

Simultaneously, coding-agent capability is commoditizing into open source. September 1’s Product Hunt slate featured Kilo Code — a fully native, open-source coding agent for the JetBrains IDE family — alongside an open-source GPU price index (CGI) and an open-source terminal manager (Nodeterm).

Read together: the ability to write code is falling toward free and open source, while the ability to make a fleet of agents deliver reliably and auditably inside an enterprise is climbing toward tens of billions. As a solo operator you belong at neither end. You belong in “one specific industry’s specific workflow” — the slot where no open-source drop-in exists and where a $47B company will not send anyone.

Distribution moved from deep pages to the front door

AI referral volume is still modest: about 1.08% of total sessions in the largest dataset (13,770 domains, 3.3 billion sessions), with IT and tech reaching ~2.8%. But the structure is what should change your build order. Within trackable LLM referrals, ChatGPT holds roughly 92%; Claude grew 64x in the same window and passed Perplexity, while Perplexity fell 61% from peak and Copilot lost 96% of referral volume.

The most actionable shift: ChatGPT referral landings moved from ~25% homepages to nearly 60% homepages.

In other words, the AI already read your deep pages on the user’s behalf, and then delivered a pre-informed visitor to your front door. That inverts a decade of landing-page practice. You no longer need a fan of long-tail pages each catching its own query intent. You need a homepage that closes the “who are you, who is this for, what does it cost, what is step one” loop in thirty seconds, with deep pages demoted to machine-citable fact sources.

Also: do not confuse being crawled with being paid. Crawl-to-referral ratios differ enormously by platform — some fetch thousands of pages for every visitor returned. Crawled ≠ cited. Cited ≠ clicked.

The top solo operators are rotating portfolios, not running marathons

A hard data point: Marc Lou reported $98,417 in monthly revenue at roughly 85% margin — but the number worth copying is the churn he flagged himself. Products that produced 90% of last year’s revenue now account for under 15%. TrustMRR ($44K) and DataFast ($26K) became the earners; the previous flagships are declining.

Put that beside the supply data. Monthly Show HN posts now run around 6,200, with Show HN’s share of all HN stories rising from 3.6% to 12.9%. New subscription apps ship at roughly 14,700 per month, about 7x the early-2022 rate. And the top 10% of apps grew MRR 306% year over year while the bottom 10% shrank.

The lesson is not “ship faster.” It is that product lifespan is compressing, and only two assets carry across products: your audience, and your launch-and-kill process. Treat products as replaceable parts and treat audience plus process as fixed assets. That is the part of the revenue structure that actually holds.

Build Directions

1. A trust layer for the agent supply chain (shortest path)

What: pre-install scanning plus continuous re-verification plus exportable audit reports for skills and MCP servers. The selling point is not malware detection — it is change detection. A skill that was safe last week and got altered this week is what buyers actually fear. Adjacent form: maintain a vetted whitelist for one vertical.

Stack: Claude Code or Codex to write the scanners and parsers; GitHub Actions for scheduled re-scans (free tier is enough to start); Supabase for scan snapshots and diffs; Cloudflare Workers to host remote MCP endpoints; Vercel for the report pages.

Cost / timeline: $50–120/month infra; 2–4 weeks to a chargeable version. Price at $19–49/month individual, $99–299/month team, from $500/month for regulated buyers — they are purchasing the audit trail, not the feature list.

2. Adopt abandoned MCP servers, resell as hosted remote

What: filter the registry for servers with real install counts that have stopped shipping (the 52% pool), fork, then add three things: auth and secret handling, rate limits and quotas, call logs and error observability. Offer it hosted so users skip local install entirely.

Stack: Cloudflare Workers or Fly.io for the remote MCP runtime; Stripe for metered billing; Better Auth or Clerk for tenant isolation; PostHog to see which tools actually get called (that decides what you fix next).

Cost / timeline: $20–80/month; 1–2 weeks per server. Price $29–99/month per workspace. The edge is inherited invocation habit — you are not buying traffic from zero.

3. Vertical voice operations (turn the 2¢/min gap into service revenue)

What: pick a niche where the phone is the workflow — dental and physio scheduling, HVAC and plumbing dispatch, auto and industrial parts lookup, clinic follow-up reminders. Do not sell “a voice AI platform.” Sell “your front desk never misses a call again.”

Stack: ThunderPhone (2–12¢/min, models included) or Vapi/Retell for the voice layer; bring your own Twilio/Telnyx SIP trunk to cut cost; self-hosted n8n to write call outcomes back into the customer’s CRM or ticketing; Supabase for transcripts and structured fields. Run AI-caller simulations as a regression suite before any agent touches a real human.

Cost / timeline: all-in roughly $0.05–0.15/min; first pilot in 1–2 weeks. Price at $500–2,000/month per location, or per resolved call — the latter closes faster because the buyer can compare it directly to a wage.

4. Rebuild the front door for pre-decided AI-referred visitors

What: convert the homepage from brand statement to answer page — positioning, who it is for, price, first step, all above the fold and machine-citable. Demote deep pages to structured fact sources (llms.txt, entity pages, quotable data points). Prove it on your own product first, then sell the same work to other builders in your niche.

Stack: Astro or Next.js for static answer pages; structured data plus llms.txt; GA4 segmented by referral source, plus a small scheduled script that asks the major models “what tool would you recommend for X” and records whether you get named.

Cost / timeline: $0–30/month; homepage rework inside a week. Sell as a $800–2,500 one-time rebuild or $99–299/month visibility monitoring.

5. Turn portfolio rotation into an operating system

What: stop optimizing for a single long-lived product. Build a line: 4–7 days to a chargeable MVP, 30 days to read retention and payment, kill it if it misses the threshold and migrate the audience to the next one. The asset is the list and the threshold, not any product.

Stack: Claude Code or Cursor for the build; Lovable or v0 for landing pages and front-end prototypes; Supabase plus Stripe for backend and payments; Resend to accumulate the list; PostHog or Plausible for retention curves. Write the kill criteria into a fixed template before you start, so the decision is not emotional.

Cost / timeline: $60–200/month in tooling; 4–7 days build plus 30 days validation per cycle. The real cost is not money — it is whether you will actually shut down something you just built because the number said so.

Risk Factors

  • The right to vet becomes the new gatekeeping fee. If 27% of public add-ons already get filtered and these whitelists become default enterprise gates, a third party decides whether your skill is reachable. That is a new platform dependency, not a solved problem.
  • Registry distribution is not owning the customer. On marketplaces and registries the platform sits between you and the buyer; you may never learn who they are. A 70–85% revenue share looks generous until you notice the platform controls exposure and terms.
  • Wherever capability commoditizes, price wars follow. Coding agents are now open-source IDE plugins; voice is compressing toward 2¢/min. Anything reproducible from the same scaffolding gets ten clones next quarter. Pricing power has to come from proprietary data, customer relationships, and taking responsibility for outcomes.
  • Voice and outbound compliance is a precondition, not a feature. Recording retention, HIPAA/GDPR, outbound calling rules, and clear liability for what the agent says are gates in regulated verticals. Do not discover this after signing.
  • AI referral data is structurally distorted. A large share of AI-driven visits arrive without referrer data, and most published benchmarks come from vendors selling into the category with no independent audit. Instrument your own baseline, segmented by assistant, and bet on that instead of somebody’s industry average.

Action Recommendations

If you are early (no stable revenue yet):

  • ✅ Do exactly one thing this week: pull 5 abandoned-but-installed MCP servers from the public registry, fork the one you understand best, and add auth plus call logging.
  • ✅ Do not publish a generic skill. Pick one industry where you have real experience and bring its 3–4 required tools up to an auditable standard.
  • ✅ Convert your homepage into an answer page: positioning, audience, price, step one, all visible immediately. The AI already read your content — the landing point is your front door.
  • ✅ Start accumulating an audience before you have a product. It is the only asset in the current revenue structure that does not decay with the product.

If you already run a mature product line:

  • ✅ Ship an official skill or MCP server for your own product and maintain it to the production bar. It is the cheapest distribution channel available right now, and most of your competitors have not done it.
  • ✅ Put auditability into your product docs: version contracts, changelogs, scan status. That is what gets you onto a regulated buyer’s shortlist.
  • ✅ Audit your revenue concentration. The reference case: a product line carrying 90% of revenue fell below 15% within a year. Prepare the next curve before the decay arrives, not after.
  • ✅ Calendar every “introductory pricing through [date]” clause you depend on, and budget the year at standard rates.

What It Means

Every layer that can be copied from a template is deflating — code generation, voice minutes, agent scaffolding. Every layer that requires someone to stand behind a result is inflating: continuous verification, industry-specific judgment, and the willingness to be accountable when an agent gets it wrong.

That split is unusually favorable to one person. You cannot outspend a lab on models and you cannot out-distribute a marketplace. But you can be the one who keeps four tools in one industry correct, documented and re-verified every week — and in an ecosystem where 52% of the supply is already dead, that is a defensible position bought with maintenance discipline instead of capital.
