Same Model, Two Harnesses, 29x the Tokens — And Cursor Just Hid the Bill

Someone ran ten agent coding tasks in an Ubuntu 26.04 VM, held the model fixed at GPT-5.6 Sol, and swapped only the harness. Token consumption ranged from 172,807 to 5,073,137. The same week, Cursor removed dollar amounts and CSV export from its usage page. Your single largest variable cost is also the only one currently being taken off the dashboard.

Key Insights

The bottom line this issue: For two years the default indie strategy has been “production got cheap, so produce more.” Three unrelated datasets this week say that strategy has expired. Cheap production is no longer the edge — metered production and owned distribution are. Full-text AI detection across 14,419 self-published genre-fiction titles on Amazon, matched to daily sales through June 2026, shows quarterly selling titles grew 19.2x while quarterly revenue grew only 8.9x. Supply finally outran demand, and revenue per selling book fell across most genres. The same week, an audit of 458 real business domains found 94.8% were never named in a single AI answer. And on the cost side, the same model in a different wrapper burns 29x the tokens. One conclusion connects all three: the easier something is to produce, the less it’s worth; the less you measure something, the more it costs you.

Numbers that should change a decision:

  • 172,807 vs 5,073,137 tokens — same GPT-5.6 Sol, same ten agent tasks, ~29x between the leanest and heaviest harness. Output tokens differed only 1.4x; the gap is almost entirely context bloat
  • ~3.9x in actual dollars ($1.58 vs $6.24 per ten tasks) once cache discounts are applied. The 29x compresses hard — and you only learn that by doing the arithmetic yourself
  • 19.2x vs 8.9x — growth in quarterly selling titles vs quarterly revenue, Amazon self-published genre fiction, 2023–2026
  • 94.8% never mentioned — of audited sites; only 1.9% of 5,978 buying-intent answers named the business being asked about
  • 38 sites block GPTBot, only 4 block OAI-SearchBot — blocking is aimed at training crawlers while the retrieval agents that actually determine citability go unmanaged. Exactly backwards
  • $0.09 / $0.18 per 1M tokens — DeepSeek V4-Flash 0731 GA pricing, with a self-reported Terminal Bench 2.1 of 82.7. The cheap tier just became viable for production agent work

Market Trends

The cost gauge is being removed right as it becomes the biggest variable

The most valuable thing on Hacker News this week wasn’t an article — it was a table buried in a comment thread. Someone ran ten agent coding tasks in an Ubuntu 26.04 VM, fixed the model at GPT-5.6 Sol, and varied only the harness:

Harness API total Input Cached Uncached Output
smol 172,807 142,334 8,704 133,630 30,473
Pi 427,211 392,767 137,216 255,551 34,444
OpenCode 1,564,429 1,523,957 1,204,736 319,221 40,472
Codex 3,005,744 2,953,154 2,649,344 303,810 52,590
Hermes 3,856,611 3,808,231 3,167,232 640,999 48,380
Claude Code 5,073,137 5,029,969 4,587,008 442,961 43,168

That’s roughly 29x. Output tokens ranged 30,473 to 52,590 — only 1.4x. The harnesses did comparable amounts of work; the entire spread is how much each one stuffs into the context window — system prompts, skill files, tool descriptions, history replay.

But reading 29x as a 29x bill is wrong, and that’s the genuinely interesting part. Priced at published rates for Sol ($5 uncached input, $0.5 cached, $30 output per 1M), ten tasks cost:

Harness Cost per 10 tasks
smol ~$1.58
Pi ~$2.38
OpenCode ~$3.41
Codex ~$4.42
Claude Code ~$5.80
Hermes ~$6.24

A 29x token spread lands as ~3.9x in dollars. Cache discounts absorb most of it — and the most context-heavy harnesses happen to have the highest cache hit rates.

The real lesson isn’t “switch to a leaner harness.” It’s that you cannot derive the bill from the token count, or the token count from the bill, so you need to keep both yourself. Which is precisely the link Cursor severed this week — dollar amounts and CSV export removed from the usage page, a 326-point thread, and a comment section that couldn’t find a single user-positive reading of it. Meanwhile the Show HN feed produced at least six independent agent cost trackers in the same window: Agentic Metric, ObservAgent, CC Meter, Orbit, Sidekick, ClawSight. Vendor removes the gauge, users build their own. Every time that loop appears, a durable third-party category grows out of it.

Layer in the price shelf and the arithmetic changes entirely. DeepSeek V4-Flash 0731 went GA this week — 284B total parameters, 13B active, 1M context, 384K output, priced at $0.09 input / $0.18 output / $0.018 cached. Rerun the same token counts: the heaviest harness (Claude Code) costs ~$0.13; the leanest (smol) costs ~$0.018. Across harness and tier, ten identical tasks span $0.018 to $6.24 — roughly 340x.

The honest caveat: capability is not equivalent, benchmarks are self-reported, and Sol is very likely still stronger on hard tasks. But for batchable, retryable, structured agent work, 340x isn’t an optimization — it’s whether your product has a gross margin.

Discovery is broken at both ends

Hacker News produced an unusual cluster this week: “How Google helped destroy adoption of RSS feeds” at 564 points, “Google has abandoned Google News?” at 274, “A directory of people who love RSS” at 175, “Atom is better than RSS, in ways that matter” at 113. Four high-scoring posts, same day, same underlying story — power users are retreating from platform distribution and rebuilding owned channels. The Google News piece is specific: date-range filtering ignored, region filtering ignored, results flooded with social media instead of publications.

The other end hasn’t connected either. An ongoing audit spanning 458 domains, 531 site audits and 38 detected sectors put category buying-intent questions to ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and Perplexity — 5,978 answers total. Only 1.9% named the business being asked about. Just 10 of 193 sites were named even once. 94.8% never appeared. Gemini had the widest reach and still only hit 2.9%.

More actionable is the fixable portion. 8.9% of sites block at least one AI crawler in robots.txt — and the blocking is pointed the wrong way. 38 sites disallow GPTBot and 36 disallow ClaudeBot (training crawlers), while OAI-SearchBot is blocked by only 4 and Perplexity-User by 3 (the retrieval agents that actually fetch a page to answer a live question). Blocking training crawlers costs you nothing in today’s answers. Blocking retrieval agents deletes you from them. Separately, only 54.6% publish any schema.org structured data and only 19.3% publish LocalBusiness markup — in a corpus that is mostly local businesses.

What the two halves mean together: platform distribution is degrading, AI answer surfaces haven’t picked you up yet, and the only thing you can fill that gap with is an owned list. Email, a clean feed, and a page structure designed to be quotable. This isn’t nostalgia — it’s currently the only distribution asset you control outright that can’t be zeroed by an API change.

The first market to get flooded now has real data

The argument about whether AI content dilutes a market has always lacked actual sales data. A paper this week supplies it: full-text AI detection across 14,419 self-published genre-fiction books sold on Amazon from 2023 to 2026, matched to daily sales records through June 2026. None of these books disclose AI content.

The finding is more uncomfortable than “AI slop doesn’t sell.” Books with substantial detected AI text (>25%) make up a large share of the catalog and a smaller share of sales — but they reach commercial scale, win a growing share of sales over time, and take top-rank positions once held by books with no detected AI text.

The structural number to remember: over this period quarterly selling titles grew 19.2x while quarterly revenue grew only 8.9x. The market added things-being-sold more than twice as fast as it added revenue, and revenue per selling book fell across most genres. Losses were unevenly distributed: no-AI books lost the most ground in genres with high AI diffusion, and most of all where Kindle Unlimited availability was high. The more the distribution structure resembles all-you-can-eat subscription, the earlier the dilution hits.

Don’t file this as publishing news. It’s a leading sample: a market where production cost approaches zero, distribution is subscription-based, and buyers can’t assess quality before purchase will see supply explode first and unit revenue collapse second. Which lanes look like that? Template sites, small utility apps, Notion templates, prompt packs, generic GPTs, content subscriptions, AI stock-asset libraries. Software’s version of this is running — nobody has just done the 14,419-sample detection on it yet.

Build Directions

1. A spend controller that lives on the gateway (not another dashboard)

What to build: A proxy between your agent and the model API that logs every request by token tier (uncached input / cached input / output), converts to dollars at current published rates, attributes by project and task, and enforces hard budgets that terminate rather than alert. The critical feature is spanning multiple harnesses and multiple providers so it can answer: what did this same task set actually cost under that combination?

Why it sells now: Vendors are pulling this data back (Cursor removed usage dollars and CSV export this week) while harness choice alone creates 29x token / ~3.9x cost dispersion. Both happening at once means asking the vendor for line items is a closing door, and the proxy position is the only stable observation point.

Stack: An OpenAI-compatible reverse proxy is the whole architecture — LiteLLM or hand-rolled. Postgres for line items, DuckDB for aggregation, a daily scrape of public pricing pages. Implement hard budgets as prefunded spend keys — physically incapable of overrunning, which is worth ten times a soft alert.

Cost & timeline: 3–4 weeks to a usable v1. The surface area is genuinely small. Honest risk: at least six indie developers shipped versions of this in the same week. Building “another cost dashboard” is dead on arrival. Only two differentiators survive: cross-harness comparison (nobody does it, because it requires running benchmarks rather than reading logs), or spend keys that actually block (requires payments and quota plumbing, which is annoying, which is exactly why it’s a moat). Price $29–$99/mo solo, $299+/mo teams.

2. AI answer visibility — fix it, then prove it

What to build: Not another SEO report. A deliverable fix list with before/after verification. Step one: check whether robots.txt is accidentally blocking retrieval agents (OAI-SearchBot, Perplexity-User, ChatGPT-User) — the most common and most fatal misconfiguration. Step two: add Organization / LocalBusiness / Product structured data. Step three: run a fixed set of category buying-intent prompts across four models for a baseline, rerun two weeks post-fix, and deliver a mention-rate curve.

Why it sells now: 94.8% never mentioned means the market is almost entirely empty. And the 38 : 4 ratio says a large number of sites are blocking themselves — a problem that takes tens of minutes to fix and produces an attributable result. That’s the exact shape of a service you can charge for and actually deliver. With only 19.3% of local businesses publishing LocalBusiness markup, the supply gap is just as wide.

Stack: A crawler for robots.txt and homepage markup; JSON-LD templates for structured data; scheduled runs across four model APIs with entity extraction for measurement. The real asset is the per-vertical prompt corpus — the code is a weekend.

Cost & timeline: 4–5 weeks to something sellable. Prices well as a one-time audit at $300–$800 plus $49–$149/mo monitoring. Target local service businesses — clinics, law firms, contractors, veterinary, education agents. They already pay for Google rankings, so the budget and the sales language both exist, and this cohort will essentially never do it themselves.

3. Tier down everything that can be tiered (margin work, not a new product)

What to build: Not a product — a gross-margin decision on the product you already have. Split tasks into three classes: needs frontier reasoning, structured-and-retryable, pure format transformation. Route each to flagship / cheap / minimal tiers. Measure before migrating, against your own task set rather than published benchmarks.

Why it works now: The cheap tier crossed into viability. DeepSeek V4-Flash 0731 posts Terminal Bench 2.1 = 82.7, Toolathlon verified = 70.3, DeepSWE = 54.4 at $0.09 / $0.18, with 284B total parameters and 13B active. For reference:

GPT-5.6 Sol $5.00 $30.00 $0.50
Claude Opus 5 $5.00 $25.00 $0.50
Kimi K3 $3.00 $15.00 $0.30
Gemini 3.1 Pro $2.00 $12.00 $0.20
Claude Sonnet 5 $2.00 $10.00 $0.20

DeepSeek V4-Flash $0.09 $0.18 $0.018

The flagship-to-cheap spread is now 50x+, not the 3–5x it used to be.

Stack: LiteLLM or your own router. The only real work is building the eval set first — pull 50 to 100 real tasks from your logs and write pass criteria. Cache strategy has to change with the tier: cheap-tier discount structures differ, and porting flagship-tuned context design over will surrender half the savings.

Cost & timeline: 2–3 weeks to a decision grounded in real load, longer to production. The honest outcome may be “not worth it” — if retry cost on failures exceeds the token delta, stay on flagship. But most teams have never run this split and are paying flagship rates for format conversion.

4. Rebuild owned distribution — for other people

What to build: Infrastructure-as-a-service for small publishers, niche blogs and industry consultants who have content but depend on platform traffic: a clean Atom feed, an email list, and a page structure designed to be cited — explicit entity declarations, stable URLs, crawlable body text, clear update timestamps.

Why now: The platform side is degrading (Google News date and region filters repeatedly reported broken), and the AI answer side hasn’t picked up the slack (94.8% unmentioned). That gap is turning owned subscriber lists back into a scarce asset, and four high-scoring RSS threads in one week on Hacker News is demand surfacing.

Stack: A static site generator with a proper Atom feed; Listmonk self-hosted or a low-cost service like Buttondown for email; reuse the structured-data templates from direction 2. The technical bar is near zero — the product is process and standards, not code.

Cost & timeline: 2–3 weeks to a templated delivery flow. $500–$2,000 per project plus a hosting retainer. State the limit plainly: this is a services business, and the ceiling depends entirely on whether you can template the delivery. If you can’t, it’s freelancing.

5. Go where the flood can’t reach

What to build: A selection principle, not a project. The 14,419-book study yields a reusable test: any market where production cost approaches zero, distribution is subscription/unlimited, and buyers can’t assess quality before purchase will see supply explode and unit revenue collapse. Go find places that break at least one of those three.

How to find them: Markets requiring proprietary data (a source others can’t get); requiring real-world action (phone calls, site visits, offline system integration); requiring accountability (someone is liable when it’s wrong, so buyers pay for traceability); or priced on outcomes (more supply can’t dilute your price because price is anchored to the result).

Stack: N/A — this determines where you point directions 1 through 4. The only operational move: run the three-part test before starting anything new. If all three hit, you’re investing time in a structure that has now been empirically measured collapsing.

Risk Factors

  • The cost-tracker lane was already crowded this week. Six indie launches in one window means the pain is real and the barrier is nonexistent. Only “blocks, doesn’t just display” or “compares across harnesses” survives.
  • 29x tokens is not 29x the bill. Cache discounts compress it to ~3.9x. Anyone pitching savings straight off token counts either hasn’t done the math or is selling something.
  • Cheap-tier benchmarks are self-reported. Terminal Bench 82.7 has no independent replication. Run your own task set before production, and watch retry rates specifically — a cheap model that needs three attempts costs more than an expensive one that lands first try.
  • AI visibility is a moving target — don’t sell it as a channel. A model update can swing mention rates while you change nothing. Position it as “fix misconfiguration plus ongoing monitoring.” Promise ranking outcomes and you’ll be issuing refunds in month one.
  • The dilution finding comes from one category. Self-published genre fiction has a very specific distribution structure (Kindle Unlimited, unlimited-read subscription). Extrapolating straight to SaaS distorts it. Treat it as a leading indicator, not a verdict.
  • Owned-channel services have a low ceiling. Direction 4 is a services business. Without templated delivery it’s trading hours for money, and the scale path needs to be settled before the first client.

Action Recommendations

If you’re just starting out:

  • ✅ Run three real tasks tonight through your usual harness and record the token tiers. Ten minutes of data determines your largest variable cost for the next year, and most people have never looked
  • ✅ Run twenty category buying-intent prompts across four models with your own product name and count the mentions. A baseline of zero is normal — 94.8% are zero
  • ✅ Check robots.txt right now for accidental blocks on OAI-SearchBot, Perplexity-User and ChatGPT-User. Five-minute job; getting it wrong deletes you from every AI answer
  • ✅ Before starting anything new, run the three-part test: zero-cost production, subscription distribution, quality unassessable before purchase. All three hit means pick a different problem

If you already have something running:

  • ✅ Pull 50 real tasks from your logs into an eval set and run them on both flagship and cheap tiers, comparing pass rate and total cost. The spread went from 3–5x to 50x+, so this answer is probably different from your judgment six months ago
  • ✅ Convert budgets from alerts to termination. A soft alert is a log line; a prefunded spend key is control
  • ✅ Audit whether your revenue depends on one platform’s distribution. If it does, build the email list this year — Google News filters have been broken for months and no one is accountable for it
  • ✅ Check whether your product trips the three-part test. If it does, the move isn’t more features — it’s shifting one notch toward proprietary data, real-world action, or outcome-based pricing

What It Means

Put the three threads side by side and they describe one shift: the last three years of indie leverage came from cheaper production. This round separates people on measurement and distribution.

The book market already ran the full experiment. When production cost hits zero, distribution is unlimited-read subscription, and buyers can’t assess quality upfront, the market adds inventory twice as fast as it adds revenue and unit economics collapse. That structure exists in software too — nobody has just run the detection on it yet.

On the cost side, the largest variable was removed from the interface the same week its dispersion turned out to be wide enough to eat a business: $0.018 to $6.24 for the same ten tasks. You can’t optimize a number you can’t see, and seeing it now requires building your own instrument.

The good news is that both of these favor small operators. Installing gauges, fixing robots.txt, running eval sets, building an email list — none of it needs a research team. It needs someone willing to actually do the arithmetic once. The hard part got commoditized; what’s left is the boring part people will pay for.
