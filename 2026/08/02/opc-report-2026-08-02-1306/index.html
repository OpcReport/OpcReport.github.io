Your Agent Bill Is 25× Your Estimate — And 12× What You Could Be Paying

Your Agent Bill Is 25× Your Estimate — And 12× What You Could Be Paying

Two unremarkable things showed up on Hacker News this week. Put together, they point at the same thing.

The first was a research write-up arguing that AI’s real threat to jobs isn’t job loss — it’s lower paychecks. Not mass unemployment. Wage compression. The work stays; the price of the work falls.

The second was a Show HN: a site tracking live API pricing across 232 models, which did something almost nobody has done carefully — converted token prices into “what will this actually cost my business per month.” Its opening example is enough to make you sit up. A support agent handling 200 tickets a day, estimated the obvious way (one request per ticket), predicts about $100/month. The real bill is about $2,530/month. Off by 25×.

Connect them. On one side, the price you can charge for delivered work is being compressed. On the other, your cost to deliver can drop by an order of magnitude at identical quality — but only if you have actually measured it.

The last cycle was won by whoever could get distribution. This one is won by whoever knows what each unit of delivery truly costs, and what it could cost instead.

Most one-person AI products are priced against a cost baseline nobody ever measured. That is not a frugality topic. That is the question of whether the margin is positive.

Key Insights

  • 25× underestimate. An 8-step agent at 200 tasks/day: naive estimate ~$100/month, actual ~$2,530/month. A task that feels like one request is 15 requests with compounding context.
  • 12× reduction available. The same agent: ~$790/month with caching, under $200/month after routing 6 of 8 steps to a mini model — with the same task success rate on routine tickets. That is not a savings tip. That is pricing headroom.
  • Agents cost 10–30× more than chatbots. Typical tasks run 5–20 steps; complex coding or browsing runs 15–50+. Long-tail tasks dominate the bill, so read the step distribution, not the average.
  • 4.7× spread on identical silicon. H100 on-demand: $1.49/hr on a peer marketplace, $6.98/hr at a hyperscaler. Provider choice beats any discount program.
  • 10–50× spread per image. $0.003 on an aggregator-hosted open-weight model, up to $0.17 premium. At 10,000 images/month: $30–100 vs $1,200–1,700.
  • ~111× spread on flagship input pricing — $0.09 to $10.00 per million input tokens across current models. “Which model” stopped being a capability question and became a business-model question.
  • 90% and 50%. Prompt caching on repeated input; batch processing on everything. Stacked, the same workload produces a different shape of bill.

Market Trends

1. Inference pricing isn’t falling — it’s diverging

“AI keeps getting cheaper” is true and it hides the part that matters more for a one-person business: the distance between cheap and expensive is now far larger than the overall price decline.

Look at this week’s live table. At the top of the flagship tier, a model bills $10.00 per million input tokens and $50.00 output. Near the bottom of the same table, a capable value model bills $0.09 / $0.18. That is roughly 111× on input and 278× on output. The middle is crowded too — $2.00/$10.00, $2.50/$15.00, $2.00/$12.00, $1.25/$2.50, $0.42/$1.32 — a dozen models fighting inside one order of magnitude.

Tier Input / 1M Output / 1M Use it for
Premium flagship $5–$10 $25–$50 Planning, hard reasoning, final judgment
Mid flagship $1.25–$2.50 $2.50–$15 Default production reasoning
Value tier $0.09–$0.45 $0.18–$1.32 Parsing, tool selection, formatting, volume

Image generation diverges harder. Premium proprietary runs $0.04–$0.17 per image, hosted open-weight $0.02–$0.10, and fast open-weight models on aggregators as low as $0.003–$0.01. Equivalent-quality image generation got roughly 25–40× cheaper over 18 months. That sentence has a direct consequence for anyone who once ran the numbers on a feature, shook their head, and shelved it: a unit economic model that failed a year ago may work now. Re-run it before you assume.

GPU rental behaves the same way.

H100 (80GB) route From ($/hr) ≈ Monthly (8h × 22d)
Peer marketplace $1.49 $262
GPU cloud $2.39–$2.99 $421–$526
Hyperscaler $6.20–$6.98 $1,091–$1,228

That 4.7× is not explained by service quality. You are paying for compliance certifications, enterprise SLAs, networking and existing contract credits — and most one-person companies need none of the four.

There is a counterintuitive threshold worth committing to memory: below roughly 40–60% utilization, calling an API is almost always cheaper than renting a GPU. “Self-hosting saves money” only becomes true above a utilization level most early products never reach. Run the API bill for three months before you argue about self-hosting. Related: a $0.35/hr consumer card (~$62/month at 8h × 22d) handles most image generation and small-model inference that people reflexively rent H100s for.

2. Agent cost structure is not what you think it is

This is the mechanism worth remembering. Agent bills surprise teams by 10–30× for three compounding reasons:

  • One task is many calls. Simple lookups: 2–4 steps. Research or multi-tool workflows: 5–15. Complex coding or browsing: 15–50+.
  • Every step resends the full context — system prompt, tool schemas, and a growing action history. By step 8, resent context has grown to roughly 7,000 tokens, and the last step alone costs more than the first three combined.
  • Failed steps trigger retries, and retries appear in nobody’s estimate.

So the “one task, one request” mental model lands 25× low. But the flip side is the good news: because cost concentrates in a repeatedly resent static prefix, the optimization leverage is unusually large. In order of impact:

  1. Prompt caching. The static prefix is resent every step; caching typically cuts agent input cost 50–70%.
  2. Route by difficulty. Mini models handle tool selection, formatting and parsing at 10–30× lower cost; the flagship handles planning and hard reasoning only. The production pattern that keeps recurring: mini models carry 70–90% of steps. Pure-flagship agents are the single most common source of runaway bills.
  3. Trim tool schemas. Every unused tool definition is paid tokens on every step.
  4. Cap max steps and add early-exit conditions. Long-tail tasks dominate cost; truncating the tail truncates the bill.

The mistake that costs thousands: caching matches from the first token forward and breaks at the first difference. Put a timestamp, a random request ID, or anything that varies at the top of your prompt and you have silently disabled caching on every request — no error, no warning, just a multiplied bill. Correct order: static first (system prompt, instructions, tool definitions, few-shot), semi-static next (retrieved docs, user profile), dynamic last (newest user message). Ten-minute change, potentially half the bill.

3. Wages are compressing, not disappearing

The research discussed this week reframes AI’s labor impact: the real shock is downward pressure on pay, not unemployment. The jobs exist, the work exists, but the same output sells for less.

For a one-person business this cuts two opposite ways, and you have to hold both.

The bad half: if you sell human execution by the hour — copywriting, video editing, basic development, data cleanup — you are standing on the compressed side. Clients get more for the same money, so they will pay less. Working harder does not reverse this.

The good half: pricing power is moving from “hours invested” to “knowing what to do, and how to deliver it at a tenth of the cost.” That 12× reduction above came with unchanged delivery quality. The gap is the new margin. Whoever owns routing, caching and model selection can produce positive margin under the client’s expected price. Whoever doesn’t is running formatting steps on a flagship model and wondering why the product loses money at scale.

One warning: do not read this as “compete on price.” Handing the entire cost advantage to the client is donating your margin in advance. The correct move is to trade cost advantage for a change in billing model — from hourly to per-outcome, per-usage, or flat monthly. The client gets a fixed price and a fixed deliverable; you keep a cost structure they neither see nor need to.

4. Tooling is finally filling in the measurement layer

What is notable this week is not only the prices, but that someone started taking pricing observability seriously. A site tracking 232 models live, with dedicated calculators for agent cost, RAG cost, voice cost, image cost, GPU rental comparison and token counting, appeared on Show HN. In the same batch of new projects: an open-source coding agent for IDE/CLI/SDK, a three-layer 19-agent parallel research workflow, and assorted “start a business with one person” tools.

Individually these are small. Collectively they mark a shift: the market has moved from “can AI do this?” to “what does doing it once actually cost?” — and that second phase is historically the one a one-person company is best at, because it rewards arithmetic and craft rather than capital and headcount.

Build Directions

Direction 1 — AI unit-economics dashboard and audit (strongest signal)

What to build: Turn “what does this AI feature cost per customer, per request, per task” into a chart someone can look at — then bring the number down. Sell the audit first, productize it second.

Layer Tool Notes
Instrumentation OpenTelemetry + LLM semconv, or Langfuse / Helicone (self-hostable) Tag token usage by feature, customer, trace. This step is where all the value comes from, and almost nobody has done it
Attribution Postgres / ClickHouse over traces Aggregate true unit cost per customer
Price source Own refreshed model price table from public provider pages Converts tokens into dollars
Reporting Current cost / optimized cost / delta The comparison sheet is the renewal argument

Cost & timeline: $20–60/month infra; 3–4 weeks to a sellable package.
Pricing: $1,500–4,000 one-time audit; $5,000–15,000 audit plus implementation (optionally a share of the first three months saved); $300–900/month for ongoing monitoring.
Why now: a 25× underestimate and a 12× optimization gap existing simultaneously means nearly every team running an AI feature has recoverable money in its bill. And the result verifies itself — next month’s invoice is the acceptance test. No narrative required.
The honest risk: if you price on savings share, the client may leave once optimization is done. Sell monitoring and regression (engineers will break your caching with a prompt edit) as a separate line item, never as a bonus.

Direction 2 — Agent cost retrofits (routing + caching, narrow and repeatable)

What to build: A deliberately narrow, highly repeatable engagement: go in, convert a client’s all-flagship agent into tiered routing plus caching, and cut the bill by an order of magnitude at unchanged quality.

Layer Tool Why
Routing LiteLLM or OpenRouter as a gateway Split by step type: mini for parsing/tool-selection/formatting, flagship for planning
Caching Provider-appropriate config Automatic caching needs prefix discipline; explicit caching needs breakpoints and write-cost math
Batch Batch API for anything non-realtime Digests, bulk classification, offline enrichment — straight 50%
Guardrail Fixed regression suite on real tasks Report “cost down X×, success rate ±Y%”

Cost & timeline: near-zero infra; 2–3 weeks to a repeatable process.
Pricing: $3,000–10,000 per retrofit, or 20–30% of first-year savings; $200–600/month for the regression harness.
Why now: this is the only direction here where payback can be measured in weeks. The client doesn’t have to believe any trend forecast — they compare two invoices. And the prevalence of the all-flagship mistake means prospect density is unusually high.
The honest risk: you are touching someone’s production agent. Demand real task samples, build the regression set, run shadow-mode comparison, then shift traffic. The savings never outweigh one bad break.

Direction 3 — Convert cost advantage into a billing model (outcome-priced micro-services)

What to build: Pick a category being wage-compressed — content production, data cleanup, basic design, routine support — and refuse to sell it by the hour. Productize it: fixed price, fixed deliverable, fixed turnaround. Your cost structure eats the spread.

Stack: value-tier or open-weight models by default, escalating to premium only for final assets and brand-sensitive steps (the same $0.003-default / $0.04-on-demand logic that works for images) → a plain queue plus state machine for orchestration, with hard step caps (you rarely need a heavy agent framework) → sampling, rule-based validation and human final review, with human time spent only on the last 10% → the client sees one price and one delivery date, never the model choice.
Unit cost: typically lands at 5–15% of sale price, depending on category.
Pricing: per-unit (per piece, per image, per ticket) or flat monthly. Anchor below what the client used to pay for labor and above your true cost — and that band is unusually wide right now.
Why now: wage compression pushes the client’s expected price down; price divergence pushes your cost down faster. The gap opening between those two lines is this cycle’s profit zone for one-person companies. It is temporary — as routing knowledge spreads, the gap narrows.
The honest risk: this direction slides into price competition easily. The defense is picking a niche with a high quality-judgment bar and a clear cost of error, not a generic category. In a generic category you only have a cost advantage; in a niche you also have a judgment advantage.

Direction 4 — Compute routing advisory (API vs GPU cloud vs self-host)

What to build: Answer one concrete question for teams already burning an AI bill — “for this workload, should we keep calling the API, move to a GPU cloud, or self-host?” — then execute the migration.

Stack: compute real utilization first (below 40–60%, the API essentially always wins; above it, proceed to comparison) → price the same GPU across marketplace, GPU cloud and hyperscaler tiers, where a 4–5× spread is public and checkable, plus 30–70% off for spot/interruptible → right-size the card, downgrading workloads from reflexive H100 rentals to consumer cards where appropriate → deploy on vLLM or SGLang with checkpoint-friendly batch jobs suited to interruptible instances.
Cost & timeline: 2–4 weeks per engagement.
Pricing: $2,500–8,000 for assessment plus migration; $300–800/month for capacity and cost monitoring.
Why now: the 4–5× same-hardware spread is a published fact, not a forecast. You are selling a document showing what they overpay, why, and how to fix it. Persuasion cost is close to zero.
The honest risk: marketplace pricing is cheap because host reliability and uptime vary. Only put interruptible, checkpointable, retryable workloads there; keep production inference on a reliable tier. Write that constraint into the deliverable — it protects you.

Risk Factors

  • Cost optimization is a one-time dividend, not a moat. This routing-and-caching knowledge will be common within a quarter or two, and the savings will get competed away to clients. Use the dividend window to build customer relationships and delivery assets, not a price advantage.
  • Caching and routing fail silently. One prompt edit or tool-schema update can zero out your hit rate with no error surfaced. Optimization without continuous monitoring reverts within months.
  • Never downgrade routing without a regression suite. “Same success rate on routine tasks” holds on that task distribution, not automatically on yours. Measure, then downgrade, and always keep the escalation path.
  • Price tables move faster than you expect. Any model priced against today’s rates must survive a unilateral vendor change. Keep the model layer swappable; never hardcode one vendor into business logic.
  • In finance, health and tax, liability binds before cost does. Research is explicit that general models handle steady state competently but fail on shocks like unemployment and allow portfolios to drift, with advice quality varying by how the question is phrased. Deterministic calculation, mandatory human escalation and up-front disclosure are product requirements, not polish.
  • Savings that don’t become pricing power are just savings. Most operators will use a 12× cost advantage to do the same thing for the same money. The real opportunity is building what was not viable under the old cost structure.

Action Recommendations

If you’re starting out:

  • ✅ Check the first line of your prompt today. If there is a timestamp, random ID, or anything that varies per request at the top, move it to the bottom. Ten minutes, potentially half your bill.
  • ✅ Cost the workload by real step count before writing more code — average steps × resent context per step × retry rate, not “one request per task.” If that number breaks the product, knowing now is far cheaper than knowing after launch.
  • ✅ Default to the cheap route and make the expensive model an on-demand upgrade. Images at $0.003 by default, premium only when the user clicks “enhance.” Same logic for text. Most users cannot tell.
  • ✅ Re-run the numbers on the feature you shelved a year ago. Equivalent-quality image generation got 25–40× cheaper in 18 months.
  • ✅ Do not rent a GPU. Below 40–60% utilization the API is cheaper, simpler, and easier to switch away from. Getting pushed past that threshold is a good problem to have later.

If you’re already shipping:

  • ✅ Tag token usage by customer and by feature this week. You cannot optimize a bill you can only see in total. This usually surfaces a “one feature eats 70% of cost” conclusion immediately.
  • ✅ Measure your agent’s step distribution, not its average. Long-tail tasks dominate cost and the mean hides them. Find the longest 5% and give them caps and early exits.
  • ✅ Downgrade the six of eight steps that can be downgraded, then run regression. Target 70–90% of steps on mini models. Record the before/after success rate — it is both your safety line and your first case study.
  • ✅ Move everything non-realtime to batch. Digests, bulk classification, offline enrichment, pre-generated content — a straight 50% at near-zero implementation cost.
  • ✅ Make cost per unit delivered a monthly metric, not total spend. It is the only basis on which you can cut price, change billing model, or take on a larger client.
  • ✅ Check whether your billing is still anchored to hours. If so, this is the window to move to outcome or usage pricing. Your cost structure already changed; your price structure should follow.

Quick Data Reference

  • 25× gap between naive estimate and real agent bill ($100/mo vs $2,530/mo at 8 steps × 200 tasks/day) — recost by real step count before launch.
  • 12× reduction available on the same agent (caching → $790/mo; plus tiered routing → under $200/mo) at unchanged routine success rate — this is recoverable margin.
  • 10–30× — agents vs chatbots, and mini vs flagship per step. Routing is the single largest lever.
  • 90% / 50% — prompt caching on repeated input, batch on everything. Get the prefix order wrong and the first discount silently becomes zero.
  • 4.7× — same H100 across providers ($1.49–$6.98/hr), plus 30–70% off for interruptible instances.
  • 40–60% utilization — the threshold below which the API beats renting. The one number to compute before any self-hosting debate.
  • 10–50× per-image route spread — $30–100 vs $1,200–1,700 for 10,000 images/month; image cost fell 25–40× in 18 months at equivalent quality.
  • ~111× flagship input spread — $0.09 to $10.00 per million input tokens. Model choice is a business-model decision.

What It Means

The reflexive reading of cheaper inference is “great, my costs are going down.” That is the wrong lesson, because it is happening to everyone simultaneously and therefore differentiates nobody.

The useful reading is that prices are diverging, not just falling — and divergence rewards knowledge in a way that a uniform price drop never does. A 111× spread on input tokens, a 50× spread per image, a 4.7× spread on identical silicon, a 25× estimation error and a 12× optimization gap are not five facts. They are one fact stated five times: the range of what the same work can cost is now enormous, and where you land inside that range is a decision, not a market condition.

Meanwhile the price of the work itself is being compressed. Wages down, cost-to-deliver down faster, with a widening gap in between. Everything in this issue lives in that gap. Measure the bill, route the steps, cache the prefix, right-size the hardware, then charge for the outcome rather than the hour — and keep the difference.

The operators who lose this cycle will not lose because they picked the wrong model. They will lose because they never knew what they were paying, priced against a number they invented, and discovered the margin was negative only at scale.
