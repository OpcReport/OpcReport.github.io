Same Task, Same Quality, 8x the Bill: The Harness Is Now the Product

Same Task, Same Quality, 8x the Bill: The Harness Is Now the Product

August 1, 2026 | For independent developers and solopreneurs

Key Insights

Cursor gave an agent swarm the 835-page SQLite manual and told it to build a database in Rust. No source code, no test suite, no binary, no internet. Then they ran the same task four times with different model assignments and measured what came out.

Every configuration eventually passed 100% of a held-out test suite of millions of queries. The costs ranged from $1,339 to $10,565.

That spread is the most important number published this month, because nothing about the task changed. Not the goal, not the time budget, not the quality bar. What changed was which model did the thinking and which model did the typing.

The breakdown is where it gets actionable. Workers carried at least 69% of the tokens, and over 90% in most runs. But the dollars split the other way, because planner tokens cost more. In the hybrid run, Opus 4.8 as planner produced a small fraction of the tokens and roughly two-thirds of the cost, while Composer 2.5 as worker handled the overwhelming majority of tokens for the remaining third.

Then the sentence that should reprice your entire roadmap: in the all-frontier run, the workers alone cost $9,373. In the hybrid, the entire worker fleet cost $411.

Cursor’s own explanation is the design principle: “Few moments in a large task genuinely require frontier intelligence, such as the original decomposition, the design decisions, and certain trade-offs. Once a frontier planner has collapsed the ambiguity into a detailed, explicit instruction, less expensive models simply have to follow it.”

Now put the second event next to it. DeepSeek shipped V4-Flash-0731 on July 31, and the change log is one line: they redid post-training. The architecture did not move. Same MoE, same 284B total parameters, same 13B activated, same 1M context. On DeepSWE, the score went from 7.3 to 54.4 — more than 6x. Terminal Bench 2.1 landed at 82.7. Artificial Analysis put its intelligence index at 50, one point behind GPT-5.6 Luna’s 51 — and that is Luna after the 80% cut.

Cost per task is still roughly 60% lower than Luna, because DeepSeek offers a ~98% cache-hit discount where most of the industry offers 90%. Frontend Code Arena rated it 1586, seventh overall and third among open models, at $0.14 / $0.28 per million tokens.

The person who built that agent capability came from quantitative trading, and brought a formula with him: Model + Harness = Agent. Same weights, better scheduling, six times the agent score.

Two labs, two completely different experiments, one conclusion. The model stopped being the variable. The thing wrapped around it became the variable. And the third event this week is what happens when that becomes common knowledge: YC open-sourced qm, a multiplayer agent harness, and it took 3.3k stars and 307 forks in about sixteen hours.

Market Trends

1. Planner/worker splitting is the highest-leverage optimisation available

For a year the standard cost advice has been “use a cheaper model where you can.” Cursor just quantified what that’s actually worth when you do it structurally rather than opportunistically, and the answer is roughly an order of magnitude.

The architecture is two roles, both organised around the same tree:

  • Planner agents, on the smartest available model, split a goal into pieces and delegate. A planner never implements.
  • Worker agents, on fast inexpensive models, execute those pieces. A worker never plans.

The reason this works is not parallelism. Cursor is explicit that they think the gains come from context efficiency: a single agent walking the whole tree has to hold the goal, its ancestors and its current position simultaneously, which is why long-running solo agents drift. They either focus on the immediate work and lose the plot, or hold the plot and do worse work. Split the roles and neither context ever fills with the wrong kind of detail.

That’s why this helps on moderately sized tasks too, not just 835-page ones. If you have any multi-step agent in production, this applies to you today.

Three details worth stealing verbatim:

Review is cheap relative to what it audits. Cursor tested review agents with different lenses — full transcript, output only, nothing but the codebase — and on different models with different training. No single lens catches everything, but decorrelated lenses stack, the way self-driving systems reach above-human reliability without any single perfect component. They believe stacked review was a major contributor to sustained quality.

Let agents write for their successors. They ran an experiment called the Field Guide: a folder owned entirely by the agents, whose index.md is auto-injected into every agent at start, curated by the agents themselves under a line budget. The logic is that model weights are frozen, so surprise encounters are exactly what’s worth capturing so the next trajectory is shorter. Early results were promising, and they expect larger benefits on codebases the agents don’t own.

The failure modes are organisational, not technical. At 1,000 commits per second they hit split-brain design (two planners solving the same question differently), planner contention, megafiles that choke everything, and ossification where agents refuse to touch core code because they learned not to. Every fix was a coordination mechanism: shared design docs with compile-checked references, a neutral third-party merge agent, a flag-and-decompose path for bloated files, licensed intentional breakage where the compiler propagates a change and each agent reads the comment explaining why.

One caution buried in a footnote, and it matters if you’re picking a model this week: they wanted GPT-5.6 Sol as the frontier config, but found it more sensitive to literal and emphasised wording than the others, producing runaway spirals unlike anything else they tested. Newest is not automatically best inside a harness you already tuned.

2. Post-training and caching now decide price, not parameter count

DeepSeek’s update is the cleanest natural experiment we’re going to get. Architecture frozen. Parameters frozen. Context window frozen. Only post-training changed, and DeepSWE went from 7.3 to 54.4, beating their own Pro preview.

The framing that produced it came from quant trading, where the discipline was never to throw compute at a problem but to filter signal out of noise and route precisely, making the best decision with the least computation. Applied to model scheduling, it produced a 6x jump on agent benchmarks with zero new weights.

The second half is the pricing story, and it’s the direct continuation of the caching argument that Manifest made when they killed their LLM router. DeepSeek’s cache-hit discount is ~98%, against roughly 90% elsewhere. That single engineering detail is why V4-Flash costs ~60% less per task than GPT-5.6 Luna despite scoring 50 against Luna’s 51, and despite Luna having just cut prices 80%.

Underneath it: V4’s total compute per million-token context was pushed to 27% of V3.2, with KV cache at 10%. Constrained access to top-tier silicon forced the optimisation, and the optimisation collapsed the marginal cost, and the collapsed marginal cost became the list price rather than a promotion.

For a solo builder the practical read is short. Your cache-hit rate is now a bigger lever than your model choice, and your model choice is a bigger lever than your prompt engineering. A developer running an hour-long agent task on V4-Flash reported spending under one yuan. Whatever you shelved in Q1 because the token bill didn’t work, re-run the spreadsheet.

3. Taste is being codified, and the harness is where it lives

qm is a multiplayer agent harness from YC, MIT licensed, and its framing is a market observation: “Most agents are designed like personal assistants. You can make one work for a whole company, but it quickly gets complex.” Their answer gives each employee an isolated workspace, plus collaboration in channels, group messages and projects, where each person and each room has its own scoped memory, files, keychain view, permissions, crons, web apps and durable sandbox.

That scoping list is the actual product insight. Single-tenant-per-human, multi-tenant-per-org, with permissions and secrets scoped at both levels. If you sell an agent to teams rather than individuals, that is the shape you’re competing against, and it’s now free to fork.

But the part that got the comment thread going is more interesting for anyone shipping AI-generated output. qm ships an anti-slop taste skill — an explicit, enforced design ruleset. It bans specific palettes it identifies as recurring AI tells. It states that text-only pages with fake-screenshot divs are slop. And it bans the em dash outright: “COMPLETELY banned. It is the LLM’s signature stylistic crutch and it is the #1 visual Tell in production tests. There is no ‘limited use’ allowance.”

Two reactions from the thread are both correct and both worth internalising.

The first is that this creates a new basin of tastelessness — a look that isn’t current slop but becomes slop once everyone copies the same ruleset. The second, sharper, is that the skill runs to 22,069 tokens and is written almost entirely as prohibitions. One commenter’s objection is a design principle: “everything is prompted via negativa.” A rule file that only says what not to do produces output defined by avoidance, and avoidance converges.

The signal underneath the argument: taste is now a maintained artifact that ships with your harness. It has a token cost, a drift problem, and a shelf life measured in months. Nobody has solved it well. That’s a gap.

Build Directions

Five directions, each reaching a chargeable v1 inside 3–9 weeks of solo work.

Direction 1 — Planner/worker cost splitting as a managed service

What you build. A layer that takes an existing single-model agent product and splits it into frontier-planner plus cheap-worker, then shows the customer the before-and-after bill.

  • The number you sell against: Cursor’s worker fleet went from $9,373 to $411 at similar output quality. Even a fraction of that is a trivially justified purchase
  • What v1 actually does: intercept the agent loop, route decomposition and design decisions to the frontier tier, route execution to a cheap tier, enforce the rule that planners never implement and workers never plan
  • The critical constraint: the planner must collapse ambiguity into an explicit instruction before handoff. If the worker still has to decide anything, quality drops and you’ve built a cheap-model wrapper instead of a swarm
  • Stack: a proxy or SDK shim, one frontier model pinned (Opus-tier), one cheap worker model pinned (Composer-tier, or V4-Flash at $0.14/$0.28), per-call-site attribution, ClickHouse, a dashboard that shows planner cost vs worker cost separately
  • Timeline: 4–6 weeks
  • Pricing: $199–499/mo, or 10–15% of documented savings for the first year. Land on a free audit that produces the two numbers

Direction 2 — Cache-hit optimisation with cross-provider arbitrage

What you build. Last week’s cache thesis, now with a second lever: providers differ enormously in how much they reward cache hits.

  • The arbitrage, concretely: DeepSeek’s ~98% cache-hit discount against an industry norm near 90% produces ~60% lower cost per task than a competitor scoring one index point higher after an 80% price cut. The discount rate is a shoppable parameter and almost nobody shops it
  • Product: measure cache-hit rate per call site, restructure prompt assembly so the stable prefix sits in front, then model the same workload’s cost across providers at their actual cache discount rates, not their headline per-token price
  • Why the headline price misleads: on a workload that is 80% repeated prefix, a provider with 98% cache discount at a higher list price beats a cheaper provider at 90%. Nobody’s pricing page shows you this
  • Stack: OpenTelemetry-compatible middleware, a cost model per provider including cache tiers, a report. No ML
  • Timeline: 3–5 weeks, the smallest build here
  • Pricing: $2–5k one-off audit converting to $99–299/mo monitoring

Direction 3 — Agent institutional memory, productised

What you build. Cursor’s Field Guide as a maintained product: a shared, agent-curated knowledge file that gets injected into every agent run and stays under a line budget.

  • The premise, in their words: model weights are frozen, so surprise encounters are what’s worth capturing so the next agent’s trajectory is shorter. Every debugging detour your agent takes twice is a line that should have been written after the first one
  • Why it’s a product and not a text file: the hard parts are curation under a budget, deduplication, staleness detection, and deciding what earns injection into every single run. Cursor notes they expect larger benefits on codebases the agents don’t fully own — which describes every consulting engagement and every open-source dependency
  • The wedge: teams running Claude Code, Codex, OpenCode or qm already have scattered AGENTS.md and CLAUDE.md files nobody prunes. Sell the pruning and the drift detection
  • Stack: git hooks, an embedding index, a cheap model doing extraction on every completed agent run, a line-budget enforcer
  • Timeline: 5–7 weeks
  • Pricing: $29–99/mo per repo. Natural bundle with Direction 1

Direction 4 — A positive-space taste layer for generated frontends

What you build. The thing qm‘s anti-slop skill gestures at but does badly: design enforcement expressed as what to do, not 22,000 tokens of what to avoid.

  • The gap, named by the critics: a rule file written entirely via negativa produces output defined by avoidance, and avoidance converges on a new uniformity. Prohibitions also cost tokens on every single call
  • What positive-space means in practice: ship an actual design system — tokens, spacing scale, type ramp, component library — and have the agent compose from it. The constraint becomes “use these primitives” instead of “avoid these fourteen palettes”
  • Why now: every solo builder shipping AI-generated landing pages has the same tell problem, and the highest-profile attempt at solving it just got publicly criticised for its approach. The problem is validated and the incumbent solution is weak
  • Bonus wedge: a token-efficiency angle. If your taste layer is 2,000 tokens against a competitor’s 22,069, you can prove the cost delta per generation
  • Stack: a token-based design system, a component registry, a compact skill file, a visual regression check that catches drift
  • Timeline: 4–6 weeks
  • Pricing: $49–149/mo, or sell as a one-off system for $3–6k to agencies who then reuse it

Direction 5 — Vertical multiplayer agents, forked from the harness

What you build. qm is MIT licensed and gives you per-person scoped memory, files, keychain, permissions, crons and durable sandboxes out of the box. Fork it and specialise for one industry.

  • Why forking beats building: the multiplayer scoping problem — one workspace per employee, shared rooms, secrets scoped at both levels — is genuinely hard and now solved in public. Your work is the domain, not the plumbing
  • Where it closes: any team of 5–50 where the agent needs different permissions per person. Accounting firms during close, clinics, law practices, property managers, agencies with per-client data walls
  • What you add: the domain workflows, the compliance posture, the integrations, and the onboarding that a general-purpose harness can never justify building
  • The market signal: 3.3k stars and 307 forks in sixteen hours says the demand for team-shaped agents is real and currently unserved by the assistant-shaped incumbents
  • Stack: the fork, one vertical’s integrations, a permissions model reviewed by someone in that industry
  • Timeline: 6–9 weeks, most of it domain work
  • Pricing: $30–80/seat/mo, or $500–2,000/mo per firm. This is the highest revenue ceiling on the list

Risk Factors

  • Newest is not best inside a tuned harness. Cursor wanted GPT-5.6 Sol as their frontier planner and fell back to GPT-5.5, because Sol proved unusually sensitive to literal and emphasised wording and produced runaway spirals. If you upgrade a model without re-tuning prompts, you can lose money and quality simultaneously.
  • Splitting planner from worker introduces a new failure class. Split-brain design, planner contention and merge collisions do not exist in single-agent systems. Cursor needed shared design docs, compile-checked references and a neutral merge agent to survive them. Budget for coordination, not just orchestration.
  • A cache-hit discount is a business decision, not a physical constant. 98% today is a strategy to buy adoption under compute constraints. Price your product on the outcome so a change in someone else’s discount rate doesn’t delete your margin.
  • Taste rules expire. The em-dash ban works precisely because it isn’t universal yet. The moment a ruleset is widely copied it becomes the next detectable tell, and one commenter has already predicted exactly that. Anything you sell as “doesn’t look AI-generated” needs a maintenance model, not a one-time delivery.
  • Prompted-via-negativa scales badly. 22,069 tokens of prohibitions runs on every call and grows every time someone spots a new tell. If your differentiator is a rule file, its token cost is your gross margin.
  • Agent-generated volume still floods human queues. 1,000 commits per second is Cursor’s number with a purpose-built VCS. Your customer’s GitHub, review process and CI were built for human tempo. Ship the throttle with the throughput.
  • Funding is not the fallback. European startup funding just hit a six-year low in deal count while AI absorbs the majority of what’s left. Build to revenue on your own timetable.

Action Recommendations

If you’re just starting out

  • ✅ Before writing any code, decide which model plans and which model works. That one decision produced an 8x cost spread at equal quality in a controlled experiment. It is the cheapest optimisation you will ever make, because it costs nothing at design time and a rewrite later.
  • ✅ Check the cache-hit discount rate of every provider you’re considering, not just the per-token price. A 98% versus 90% discount changes the ranking on any workload with a stable prefix.
  • ✅ Re-run the economics on anything you abandoned for cost reasons in the first half of this year. An hour of agent work for pocket change is a different business than it was in March.
  • ✅ Fork rather than build the plumbing. qm is MIT and hands you multiplayer scoping that would cost you two months.

If you already ship a product

  • ✅ Instrument planner cost and worker cost as separate lines this week. If they’re the same model, you have a potential 20x on the worker tier sitting unclaimed.
  • ✅ Enforce the two rules that make the split work: planners never implement, workers never plan. Half-splitting gives you the coordination overhead without the savings.
  • ✅ Add a second review lens on a different model. Decorrelated reviewers stack, and review is cheap relative to the work it audits.
  • ✅ Start a Field Guide for your own agents. One file, auto-injected, line-budgeted, written by the agents after each run. Cheapest quality improvement on this list.
  • ✅ Audit your taste layer for token bloat and negativa. If it’s mostly prohibitions, rewrite it as a design system and measure the token delta per generation.
  • ✅ If you upgraded a model recently and quality moved oddly, suspect prompt sensitivity before you suspect the model. Cursor hit exactly this and documented it.

What it means

Two labs ran different experiments this fortnight and arrived at the same place.

Cursor held the task constant and varied the model assignment, and watched the bill move 8x while the output stayed the same. DeepSeek held the architecture constant and varied only the post-training, and watched an agent benchmark move 6x. Neither result came from a better model. Both came from better arrangement.

So the scarce thing is no longer intelligence per token. It is the decomposition that turns a vague goal into an instruction a cheap model can follow without deciding anything. Cursor’s phrase for it is that the unit of work becomes the spec, and the swarm starts to resemble a compiler for intent — with the difference that a compiler preserves meaning at every step and a swarm is probabilistic at every one.

Everything worth building right now sits in that gap. Route judgment to the expensive model and volume to the cheap one. Shop the cache discount, not the sticker price. Let the agents write down what surprised them. And when you enforce taste, say what good looks like, because a rule file made of prohibitions just teaches everyone to fail in the same direction.

The model got commoditised. What you wrap around it did not.
