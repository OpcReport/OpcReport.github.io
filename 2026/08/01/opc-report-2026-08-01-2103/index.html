YC Open-Sourced the Agent Harness. The Lock-In Moved Into Your Session State.

YC Open-Sourced the Agent Harness. The Lock-In Moved Into Your Session State.

August 1, 2026 | For independent developers and solopreneurs

Key Insights

Three things landed in the same 48 hours, and they only make sense read together.

One. Y Combinator’s own software team published qm, described flatly as “a multiplayer agent harness for work. In Slack and on the web.” MIT licensed. Created July 29. As of today: 3,777 stars, 353 forks, 37 open pull requests, four tagged releases in three days. The architectural claim in its README is the part that should make you sit up:

“It’s built with open source in mind. Pick your own harness and model and switch between them — Pi, OpenCode, Codex, and Claude Code all drive the same core, so a deployment isn’t tied to any single vendor.”

Per-employee isolated workspaces, scoped memory, per-room keychain views, permissions, crons, durable sandboxes, shareable skills with admin-gated promotion. That is roughly the feature list of a dozen seed-stage “AI agent for your company” startups, published under a permissive license by the most credible startup brand in the ecosystem.

Two. On the same weekend, an engineering post titled “The session you cannot take with you” hit 751 points and 213 comments — arguing that while you were busy picking a harness, the actual lock-in moved somewhere you can’t see:

“The transcript on your machine is no longer your session but a partial view of a session whose operational state belongs to an inference provider and not you.”

The specifics are checkable. Reasoning tokens you are billed for return as opaque encrypted blobs. Hosted web search puts source material in the model’s context that your client never sees. OpenAI’s Responses API stores responses by default, retained at least 30 days. Gemini’s Interactions API also defaults to store: true — 55 days retained on paid, one day on free. Anthropic’s thinking blocks come back as a signature you cannot read, tied to the model that produced them, and the docs say to strip them when you switch models.

Three. The cost lever everyone has been chasing turns out to be the wrong one. Manifest, an open-source LLM gateway with roughly 7,000 cloud users, deprecated its own LLM router in June and shuts it down September 1 after four months in production. Their reasoning is the most useful cost engineering write-up of the week, and the punchline is short: cache reads are 75–90% cheaper than uncached input, and a cache-aware router does its job by not routing.

Put the three together and the map for a one-person company redraws itself. The layer you were going to build — the harness — is now free and vendor-neutral. The layer you did not think about — session state — is where you are being quietly captured. And the lever you were told to pull for cost — routing — is worse than the boring one you already have.

Market Trends

1. The harness is a commodity now, and it happened faster than the category could raise a Series A

For eighteen months the reflex answer to “what should I build” was some flavor of agent infrastructure: an orchestration layer, a memory layer, a Slack-native agent that knows your company. qm shipped most of that in one MIT-licensed repo, and the market voted 3,777 stars in three days.

Read the design decisions, because they tell you where the value went:

  • Multi-scope by construction. Each person and each room gets its own memory, files, keychain view, permissions, crons, web apps, and durable sandbox. That is not a feature; that is the hard part of “one agent, whole company,” and it is the part most solo builders underestimate by an order of magnitude.
  • Harness-agnostic core. Pi, OpenCode, Codex, and Claude Code all drive the same core. The abstraction that startups were selling as their moat is now the default assumption of the free option.
  • Deployment targets shipped in-repo. There are fly/, aws/microvm-agent/, deploy/, and local/ directories. The distribution problem is pre-solved for whoever wants to run it.

The same day, an independent developer published Tilde, a harness SDK built as a side project “over a year,” with a walkthrough of building a self-hosted code review agent — credential management, sandboxed checkout, MCP proxying, Slack/GitHub chat integration. His framing is worth stealing: “creating a single agent harness for all tasks both simple and highly complex would lead to sub-optimal outcomes.”

What this means concretely: stop pitching the harness. Nobody will pay for the layer YC gives away and a side project matches. The money moved to two places — above it (a specific job done end to end for a specific buyer) and beneath it (the boring plumbing the free harness assumes you already have: identity, credentials, egress control, evidence capture).

2. Provider-sealed state is the lock-in nobody priced in

The five-question test from the session portability post is the cleanest audit instrument published this month. Run it on your own product:

  1. Inspection — can you see what the model saw, what tools did, what sub-agents told each other?
  2. Export — is the session self-contained, or does it point at somebody’s database?
  3. Replay — could another implementation reconstruct a semantically equivalent context?
  4. Audit — can a human explain, after the fact, why the system took an action?
  5. Deletion — can you identify and remove every server-side copy the session depends on?

If you are building on hosted search, stored responses, and encrypted reasoning, you fail at least three of these — and you fail them silently, because the outputs look fine right up until you try to migrate, or a customer asks for an audit trail, or a regulator asks what evidence produced a decision.

The naming is the trap. encrypted_content reads like a privacy feature you control. The post’s rename is more honest: provider-sealed state. The provider holds the keys, decrypts for its own models, and decides where the data can be replayed. It does not hide the data from the provider. It hides it from you.

For a solopreneur this is not an abstract governance concern, it is a switching-cost bomb. The whole economic case for a one-person company right now is that you can move to whichever model is cheapest and best this quarter. If your sessions are pointers into a vendor’s database, you cannot actually move, and the price cuts you have been counting on stop being available to you.

3. Cost control moved from routing to caching, and the benchmark data confirms it

Manifest’s post-mortem on their own router is worth reading in full, but the three arguments transfer directly:

  • Complexity cannot be deduced from the prompt. “Evaluate the tests for this repo and improve them” is trivial for a static site and enormous for the Linux kernel. The prompt is the trigger, not the task. The information that determines cost arrives later, via tool calls.
  • Cache beats routing on pure economics. System prompts and conversation history sit at the front of the prompt, which is exactly where prefix caching works. Cache reads run 75–90% cheaper. And a cache-aware router has to add stickiness to the model it first picked — meaning it earns its keep by declining to route.
  • Switching models mid-session degrades work quality and, more subtly, detaches you from mastering your tools.

Independent benchmark data backs the caching point hard. Across agent runs on real repository tasks, the top systems report cache rates between 84.7% and 97.0%. Cache is not a micro-optimization at that ratio; it is the majority of your bill.

The same leaderboard demolishes the assumption that price tracks capability. Cursor resolves 51.7% at $0.41 per problem. Claude Code resolves 60.4% at $3.39. You pay 8.3x more for 8.7 percentage points. GPT-5.6 Sol sits at 62.3% for $0.85 — better than both on both axes in that window. There is no stable ranking here to memorize; there is only a measurement you should be running on your own task mix.

4. Unsupervised autonomy has a measurable overhead, and it is not small

A developer ran an agent unattended for fifteen days on a single instruction — prove everything — with a second AI on a different model auditing from outside. The headline finding, in his words: the proof ate the project. Five of the fifteen days produced no product code at all.

This is the honest counterweight to every “I shipped a SaaS while I slept” thread. Autonomy does not remove work, it relocates it into verification loops that you are paying for by the token and cannot see in real time. The interesting design response in that experiment is the second, differently-modeled auditor — an architecture worth copying even if the fifteen-day run is not.

Build Directions

Direction 1 — Portable session layer (transcript escrow)

What to build: middleware that captures a complete, self-contained, replayable transcript of every agent run — instructions, messages, tool calls, tool results, retrieved evidence — and stores it somewhere you control. Sell it as migration insurance and audit trail.

Stack and settings: set store: false on OpenAI Responses and store: false on Gemini Interactions so state stops living on the provider. Replace hosted search with a client-side search tool so the retrieved passages land in your log. Persist to Postgres or SQLite plus object storage. Strip Anthropic thinking blocks on model switch, as their docs instruct, and keep your own structured summary of what happened instead.

Buyer and price: any team facing an audit, procurement review, or EU AI Act documentation request. $49–199/month per workspace, or a one-time hardening engagement at $2,000–6,000. Build time: 2–3 weeks for a working v1.

Why now: the five-test framework just went viral among engineers; you are selling against a problem people can now name.

Direction 2 — Managed deployment of the free harness

What to build: a service business on top of qm. It is MIT licensed, ships Fly and AWS microVM deployment paths, and is genuinely non-trivial to operate — per-person sandboxes, keychain scoping, org-level security posture, skill promotion policy.

Stack: qm on Fly.io or AWS microVMs, your choice of harness underneath (Codex or Claude Code for coding-heavy orgs, OpenCode for cost-sensitive ones), Slack app installation, plus a hardening pass that applies the credential lessons from this week — short-lived credentials, no shared vault, egress allowlists.

Buyer and price: 10–60 person companies that want the capability without a platform team. $3,000–8,000 setup, $500–2,000/month managed. One person can carry six to ten of these.

Why now: 3,777 stars in three days means demand exists and the software is free. Adoption gap is operational, and operational gaps are what services businesses are for.

Direction 3 — Cache-rate audit and prompt restructuring

What to build: a diagnostic that measures a team’s actual cache-hit rate, then restructures prompt prefixes, system prompts, and conversation handling to raise it. Report against the observable frontier: 84.7%–97.0% cached in top agent runs.

Stack: provider usage APIs plus your own gateway logs; a small analysis script; a rewrite pass that moves everything stable to the front of the prompt and stops invalidating prefixes on every turn.

Value math: for a team burning $4,000/month on inference at a 40% cache rate, moving to 85% with reads at 80% off is roughly a $1,400/month reduction. Price the audit at $1,500–4,000 and it pays back inside a quarter. Delivery time: 3–5 days.

Why now: the router hype cycle sent everyone chasing the wrong lever, and the vendor with 7,000 users just published why. You are selling the boring thing that actually works.

Direction 4 — Per-task model selection, benchmarked on the customer’s own repo

What to build: not a runtime router — a decision harness. Run a customer’s representative task set across three or four candidate models, report cost per resolved task rather than raw token price, and hand them a fixed assignment: this model for this class of work.

Stack: an eval set drawn from the customer’s own closed issues or tickets; candidates spanning the price spectrum (a cheap agent around $0.41/task, a mid one around $0.85, a premium one around $3.39); measure resolved rate, cost per problem, and cache rate together.

Why this beats routing: it respects Manifest’s finding that complexity is not visible in the prompt, while still capturing the 8x price spread. You do the classification once, offline, with real outcomes — instead of guessing per request in production. $2,000–5,000 per engagement, repeatable quarterly as models change.

Direction 5 — Client-side search and evidence capture

What to build: a drop-in replacement for hosted search that records the query, retrieval time, ranked URLs, and actual passages fed to the model — then makes them refetchable and portable.

Stack: Brave Search API, Exa, or self-hosted SearXNG for retrieval; your own fetch-and-extract step; content-addressed storage of retrieved passages so replay is exact; expose it to the agent as an ordinary tool.

Why now: hosted search is the single largest hole in the modern transcript — the model sees source material the client never sees. Any product that needs to explain why it answered something (legal, medical, finance, procurement) cannot use hosted search and stay defensible. $29–99/month as a developer tool, or bundle it into Direction 1.

Risk Factors

⚠️ Do not build a harness. The reference implementation is free, MIT, backed by the most recognizable startup brand in the market, and shipping four releases a week. If your pitch deck’s core is orchestration, memory, and Slack integration, your differentiation window closed this week.

⚠️ Your archive may already be worthless for migration. If you have been logging user messages and final text while the reasoning, hosted search results, and sub-agent traffic stayed sealed, you have a partial view — not a session. Test this before you need it, not during a migration.

⚠️ Cheap inference is a coupon, not a moat. Cache discounts and aggressive per-token pricing are provider-side and revocable. They are the reason a one-person company works right now, and they are also the reason you must keep the ability to leave.

⚠️ Autonomy tax is real and invisible. Five of fifteen days with zero product code is what unsupervised “prove everything” looks like in practice. Budget verification loops explicitly, cap them, and always have a second model auditing from outside the run.

⚠️ EU labeling is live tomorrow. The AI Act’s transparency obligation for authentic-looking synthetic content takes effect August 2, with exposure up to 3% of global revenue. If your product generates images, audio, or video, this is now a shipping requirement, not a roadmap item.

Action Recommendations

If you are starting out (0–1):

  • ✅ Clone qm, run it locally, and read the scope and permission model. Whether or not you use it, it is the current reference design for multi-tenant agents — and studying it will save you two months of rediscovering the same problems.
  • ✅ Pick one buyer with a job that fails the five-question audit — auditability, exportability, deletion — and build for exactly them. That is a product; a harness is not.
  • ✅ Set store: false today and log your own transcripts. It costs an afternoon now and buys you the freedom to switch providers forever.
  • ✅ Measure your cache-hit rate before you optimize anything else. If it is under 70%, that is your entire cost problem.

If you already have something running (1–n):

  • ✅ Run the five-test audit on your own product this week and write the results down. If you fail export and deletion, fix those before adding features — they are the ones customers and regulators ask about.
  • ✅ Replace hosted search with a client-side tool in any flow where you may be asked to justify an output. You will get cheaper, auditable, and portable in one change.
  • ✅ Re-benchmark your model choice on your own task mix with cost per resolved task, not token price. An 8x price spread for a 9-point capability spread means the default choice is probably wrong in one direction or the other.
  • ✅ Kill any routing project you started this quarter and put that effort into prefix stability instead. The team with 7,000 users already ran this experiment for you.
  • ✅ Instrument the autonomy tax: track what fraction of agent-hours produce shipped artifacts versus verification churn. If it looks anything like 1-in-3 dead days, cap the loop.

What it means

The commoditization curve just ran through the layer most people were building on, and it took three days. That is not a reason to panic; it is a map correction. Free infrastructure raises the floor for everyone, which means the floor is no longer where you differentiate.

What is left is unglamorous and durable: owning your own evidence, keeping the ability to walk to a cheaper provider, and knowing the real cost of each task you automate. Every one of those is a boring engineering discipline rather than a clever product idea — which is precisely why they are still available to a single person with a laptop.

The harness got free this week. Make sure your sessions didn’t get expensive in the same move.
