Stars Are a Lie: What Fork Ratios Reveal About Which AI Skills Anyone Actually Uses

Stars Are a Lie: What Fork Ratios Reveal About Which AI Skills Anyone Actually Uses

August 3, 2026 — trend monitoring for independent builders and one-person companies

Key Insights

The headline judgment: Packaging expertise as a Skill is losing value from both ends simultaneously — automated away from above, disproven by data from below. What people actually adopt is not the thing that tells an agent what to do. It’s the thing that checks whether the agent did it.

On July 29 Microsoft shipped skill-recorder: record yourself doing a task, and the GitHub Copilot CLI reconstructs the session into an intent plus an ordered list of steps, then generates a SKILL.md or a scheduled Automation. It hit 1,236 stars and 127 forks in five days. The README states the pitch plainly: “recording yourself submitting one form can teach the agent to submit all of them.” The barrier to authoring a Skill just dropped to knowing how to use a mouse.

Then I ran the numbers on the trending Skill repos of the past two weeks, and the result inverts the conventional wisdom. Stars measure attention. Forks measure use. And on documentation-style Skill repos, the two are negatively correlated. ai-copywriter has 1,016 stars and a 2.4% fork ratio. SimpleEnglish has 1,395 stars and 3.7%. Meanwhile ratchet — 415 stars, far less visible — sits at 20.2%, the highest in the sample. And ratchet does the opposite of teaching an agent. It audits one.

Four numbers that should change your decisions:

  • 2.4% vs 20.2% fork ratio. The most-starred prompt collections are the least-used artifacts on GitHub right now. The most-forked one is a compliance hook. The market’s revealed preference is enforcement over advice, and it isn’t close.
  • 1,236 stars in 5 days, from Microsoft. skill-recorder outputs directly into Microsoft Scout, Copilot Cowork and Copilot Studio. When the platform gives away the Skill-manufacturing machine, individual Skills stop being scarce.
  • 929 points, 402 comments. Don’t be a meat proxy topped Hacker News today, arguing that relaying raw model output is a negative-value contribution. Alongside it: “manually retype LLM code to prevent cognitive debt” (143 pts) and “developers are attached to tools because tools encode trust” (227 pts). The week’s top engineering discourse converged on one theme — unverified AI output is not a deliverable.
  • 10–12% is the fork ratio of real software. YC’s qm at 10.5%, trycompai/crm at 12%, talivia at 10.2%, skill-recorder at 10.3%. That’s the baseline for “someone actually ran this.” Below 5% means bookmark.

Market Trends

1. Microsoft automated the Skill author

The product logic of skill-recorder deserves careful reading by anyone planning to monetize packaged expertise. The flow: record your screen (with optional spoken narration) → Copilot CLI reconstructs intent and ordered steps → you review and edit → generate a Skill or an Automation. Notably it instructs the generated output to prefer the agent’s native tools — gh CLI, web_fetch — over replaying UI clicks, and to generalize from the single example rather than hardcode it.

The implication is that the information asymmetry of “I know this workflow and you don’t” now has a much shorter shelf life. Converting tacit expertise into structured documentation used to require both capability and patience. That step is now compressed into one recording plus one review pass. Any domain veteran can export twenty years of operating habits into machine-executable steps in an afternoon — without writing Markdown, and without even being able to articulate what they do.

Pay attention to the distribution intent: all three output targets are Microsoft’s own commercial products. This is not an open-source toy. It is inventory acquisition for the Copilot ecosystem. When the platform hands out the factory for free, the individual unit produced by that factory is not a defensible asset.

What survives: two things a screen recording cannot capture. First, the judgment behind a step — why option A at step 7 and not option B, which never appears on screen. Second, maintenance — rules change, and a recording is frozen in time. If your product is a static knowledge artifact, assume it becomes a free commodity within twelve months.

2. Fork ratios expose the biggest lie in the Skill ecosystem

This is original analysis for this issue, and it’s worth spelling out the method. I took the fastest-growing agent-related repos of the past two weeks and computed forks divided by stars. A star means I saw this and it looked interesting. A fork means I’m taking this to modify and run. The ratio between them is a far better adoption signal than either absolute number, and it’s hard to game, because forking is motivated by use rather than by signalling.

The sample split into three clean tiers:

Tier Repos Fork ratio
Prompt / documentation Skills ai-copywriter (1,016★), SimpleEnglish (1,395★), old-coder (256★) 2.4% – 6.6%
Runnable software qm (8,507★), trycompai/crm (2,344★), skill-recorder (1,236★), talivia (675★) 10% – 12%
Enforcement / verification tools humanizer-cli (542★), Finn-loop (286★), andrej-karpathy-skills (549★), ratchet (415★) 12.5% – 20.2%

Read top to bottom and the conclusion is hard: the tier with the prettiest star counts has the worst adoption, and the tier with the most modest star counts has the best. Prompt collections get upvoted, retweeted, and filed into “20 AI skills worth bookmarking” listicles — then sit in the bookmark folder. Write ten rules an agent should follow and people star it. Write a hook that checks whether the agent followed those ten rules and people fork it and install it.

Take this and audit your own project. Next time you evaluate an open-source competitor — or your own traction — ignore the star count and compute fork/star. Under 5% is a collectible. Above 10% means people are running it. Above 15% means it’s embedded in someone’s workflow. It takes five minutes and can save you three months of building into a market that only exists on social media.

3. From prompts to hooks: the constraint layer is becoming its own category

The ratchet README frames the problem more precisely than most funding decks: “Every minimalism ruleset for coding agents is an open loop.” You inject prefer the standard library, do not add dependencies, keep the diff small, the model reads it, and then nothing checks whether any of it happened. Compliance is assumed. The model drifts over a long session, and nobody finds out until review.

The mechanism is a PostToolUse hook that reads every edit the agent makes, measures it, and reports findings back into the same session while the agent is still working. The worked example in the README is persuasive: the agent installs flatpickr and writes a wrapper component, the hook reports 1 new dependency, DatePicker only forwards to Flatpickr, and the agent reverts and ships a native <input type="date">. As the README puts it: “Nothing here depends on the model having felt persuaded by a system prompt.”

This is the same story as today’s HN front page from the other direction. Don’t be a meat proxy at 929 points argues that humans shouldn’t be thoughtless relays for model output. ratchet builds the machine that catches thoughtless relaying automatically. Add the 227-point piece on tools encoding trust and the 143-point argument for retyping LLM code by hand, and the four highest-signal engineering discussions this week are all about the same question: how does trust get established mechanically rather than promised verbally?

4. Founders are installing revenue dashboards, not traffic dashboards

talivia took 675 stars in five days with an unambiguous position: revenue-first, self-hosted analytics for founders — web analytics plus session replay plus revenue attribution, wired directly into Stripe, LemonSqueezy, Polar and Dodo. It positions itself as a datafast alternative and runs an open-core model with a managed cloud tier.

This meshes with everything above. When an agent can assemble a product in minutes, when Skills can be generated from a screen recording, and when GitHub stars are demonstrably a vanity metric, the only dashboard left worth watching is where the money comes from. Revenue attribution rather than pageviews becoming the default field is the entire indie community collectively revising its definition of success.

Product Hunt this week reinforced it: UniwebPay Skill (payments shipped as a Skill), Termexo (a Windows workbench for Claude Code and Codex), TimeOS 2.0, Airtop for Google Ads Automation. Tools attached to money and tools attached to hours. Not one of them is a smarter chat box.

One model-layer note: Qwen3.8-Max landed today at 729 points and 367 comments on HN, framed around coding and Cowork (professional office work). Cadence is still measured in weeks. Hardcoding a model name is actively accruing maintenance debt — route by capability tier, not by name.

Build Directions

Five directions grounded in this week’s signals, each with the stack, realistic cost and timeline, and how you get paid. Ordered from lowest barrier to highest.

Direction 1 — Build a constraint hook for one vertical, not a Skill (strongest pick this issue)

What: ratchet proved demand with a 20.2% fork ratio, but it covers exactly one dimension — code complexity — and only for general programming. Every industry with hard normative rules is missing the same-shaped tool. Openings: clinical documentation compliance (required fields and prohibited phrasing checked after the agent drafts a record or claim), accounting reconciliation checks, legal citation verification against a real case database (unusually compelling after the hallucinated-CVE incident), academic reference existence checks, accessibility rule enforcement for frontend work.

Stack: PostToolUse / Stop hook mechanisms across Claude Code, Codex and OpenCode — support all three. Deterministic validation scripts in Node or Python, and the core logic must be deterministic, not another model wrapped in a trench coat. Domain rule sets in YAML or JSON — that’s the real asset. SQLite for baseline history. Ship as an npm package with one-line npx init.

Cost and timeline: Near-zero infrastructure, $30–80/month in your own model usage while building. Usable version in 2–3 weeks.

How you get paid: Open-source the hook engine for distribution, charge for the rule sets. This is the only correct split. Rules change with regulation and industry standards, so customers subscribe to a maintained pack at $199–899/year. Enterprise tier adds audit log export — used to prove to a regulator that a verification step exists.

Direction 2 — A quality layer for the skill-recorder ecosystem

What: Microsoft turns recordings into Skills. Microsoft does not guarantee those Skills are any good. Auto-generated procedures will systematically suffer three failure modes: treating incidental actions as required steps, losing conditional branches (why was step 4 skipped that time?), and hardcoding specific values that should have been generalized. Build the lint and regression layer: static analysis over generated SKILL.md files flagging hardcoded values, missing preconditions and uncovered branches, plus regression validation against historical recordings.

Stack: Claude Code or Codex for the semantic analysis pass, deterministic linter rules (regex plus AST) for everything checkable without a model, recording diff comparison, CLI-first with a minimal web report. Target skill-recorder output format while staying compatible with hand-written SKILL.md.

Cost and timeline: Under $50/month infrastructure. MVP in 3–4 weeks.

How you get paid: Free for individuals as the distribution wedge, $29–79/month per seat for teams. The real money is enterprise: once a company starts mass-producing Skills, “who reviews these?” becomes an immediate requirement — the exact path code review tooling walked a decade ago.

Direction 3 — Revenue-first analytics for the tier Talivia skips

What: talivia validated that revenue attribution beats traffic counting, but it’s a full self-hosted platform requiring PostgreSQL and deployment, aimed at founders who already have a site and paying flow. Below that sits a much larger group: just-launched, three-figure monthly revenue, collecting through Gumroad or Lemon Squeezy, with zero interest in maintaining a database. Build the zero-deployment version: one script tag plus payment-provider OAuth, and it tells them “$31 of this month’s $47 came from that Reddit post.”

Stack: Cloudflare Workers plus D1 (cheap and global by default), webhooks and OAuth for Stripe / LemonSqueezy / Polar / Gumroad, first-touch and last-touch attribution models, and an email digest — that’s the retention mechanism, not the dashboard.

Cost and timeline: Under $30/month infrastructure; the Workers free tier goes a long way. MVP in 4–5 weeks.

How you get paid: Free until $500/month customer revenue, then $9/month. Anchor pricing to their revenue, not their traffic. “We charge once you’re earning” converts dramatically better in this segment than per-pageview metering.

Direction 4 — Verification as a service, sold to people who won’t build it

What: The hallucinated-CVE incident — a national vulnerability database issuing a CRITICAL rating against a function that does not exist — exposed a general asymmetry: generation takes seconds, refutation takes hours. That gap is a service business. Don’t build a general-purpose “AI detector”; that road ends in an accuracy dispute you cannot win. Build category-specific fact-verification pipelines: citation checking for technical whitepapers, data traceback for due diligence packets, source verification for AI-generated research, advisory authenticity checks for open-source dependencies.

Stack: Agents for first-pass triage and claim extraction (cheap tier, run at volume), a human sign-off step that you cannot remove — the signature is the product, isolated Docker containers for reproduction, structured evidence reports where every claim carries its source and verification method, Stripe for per-item billing.

Cost and timeline: Under $100/month infrastructure, but this is a labor-intensive business and early on the labor is you. First paying customer in 2–4 weeks, because the pain already exists.

How you get paid: $200–2,000 per item, or $1,500–5,000/month on retainer. You are selling accountable sign-off, not a tool run. That’s precisely why it resists automation — liability cannot be outsourced to a model.

Direction 5 — Agent workbenches for Windows and Linux

What: Product Hunt is once again saturated with macOS agent accessories — claudemon, Zen Whisper, MacDupl, yapyap. Meanwhile Termexo launched as a Windows local workbench for Claude Code and Codex into near-zero competition, Linux desktop crossed 10% market share in North America (102 points on HN), and Bor, open-source policy management for Linux desktops, pulled 176 points. These users exist, have budget, and are ignored by essentially all agent tooling.

Stack: Tauri for Windows and Linux packaging, an open harness core underneath (OpenCode, or YC’s qm — a 10.5% fork ratio says it’s solid), native tray and notification integration, tiered model routing (cheap tier for classification and triage, frontier tier only for genuine reasoning), enterprise desktop policy compatibility.

Cost and timeline: Near-zero infrastructure, $50–120/month model spend during development. Chargeable beta in 4–7 weeks.

How you get paid: Never price the agent loop — it will be open-sourced out from under you. Price the shell: native Windows integration, Linux packaging and distro support, enterprise desktop compliance. $19–49/month, per-seat for enterprise.

Risk Factors

  • Stars are a vanity metric, and a toxic one. A 2.4%–3.7% fork ratio means a repo can collect over a thousand stars while almost nobody runs it. If your validation method is “post to HN and see,” you validated your headline, not your product. Use fork ratio, paid conversion, retention — anything but stars.
  • The platform is automating the thing you planned to sell. Microsoft giving away the Skill-manufacturing machine is textbook commoditize-your-complement: they sell Copilot seats, your Skill is fuel. Any business model resting on “I have proprietary process documentation” should assume that documentation is a free commodity within twelve months.
  • Constraint tools live on someone else’s platform. ratchet depends on PostToolUse hooks owned by Claude Code, Codex and friends. They can change the protocol or ship the feature natively. The moat is never the hook — it’s the rule set, because that’s the part that keeps changing with regulation and needs a human maintaining it.
  • Verification-as-a-service is a labor business; don’t plan it with SaaS multiples. Direction 4 has far lower gross margin than software, and scaling means hiring and process standardization rather than adding servers. Cash-flow healthy and AI-resistant — but not passive income. Decide which life you want before you enter.
  • The hallucination contamination problem is spreading faster than verification capacity. Generation cost is near zero and refutation cost is measured in engineer-hours. Any institution that currently accepts submissions based on presentation quality — grant reviews, insurance claims, expert opinions, academic submissions — is exposed on the same assumption that just broke for a national vulnerability database.

Action Recommendations

If you’re pre-revenue:

  • Reclassify what you’re building this week: is it advice, or is it enforcement? If it’s advice, explain why a single screen recording can’t replicate it. The data is unambiguous — the market pays for enforcement.
  • Compute the fork/star ratio on your competitors. Five minutes, potentially saves three months. If the “hot” competitor sits at 3%, the category isn’t validated — what you saw was press, not customers.
  • Pick one vertical with hard normative rules and build Direction 1. Low barrier, short cycle, real demand, and rule maintenance creates a subscription relationship naturally. Don’t build the generic version; ratchet has that.
  • Write the sentence naming exactly what the customer pays for, before the first line of code. If the sentence is “they’ll want the pro tier,” you don’t have one.
  • Route by capability tier, never by model name. Qwen3.8-Max shipped today; something else ships next week. Keep the routing table in config so a swap is one line.

If you have a product and revenue:

  • Take the purest “knowledge” component of your product and assume it’s free next month. Then answer: what’s the rest worth? If the answer is “not much,” you need to shift the value center toward execution, maintenance or accountability this quarter.
  • Add a verification layer, not another intelligence layer. The fork data says customers are starved for certainty. A feature that states plainly “these 7 items were checked and passed” is easier to raise prices on than an eighth AI suggestion feature.
  • Wire up revenue attribution. If you’re still looking at pageviews you don’t know where to double down. talivia self-hosts in an afternoon on one small box.
  • Run skill-recorder against your own support or delivery workflow. Not to publish a Skill — to see how much of your operation is already automatable. It may be the largest labor saving available to you this year.
  • Check your Windows and Linux customer share. If your tool is macOS-only you are voluntarily abandoning a market with almost no competition.

What It Means

Last week’s read was that packaged judgment becomes the moat once distribution collapses to a repo. That still holds — but this week narrowed what “judgment” is allowed to mean. Judgment written down as a document is now manufacturable by a Microsoft desktop app from a screen recording, and the fork data says almost nobody was installing those documents anyway.

What people are installing is machinery that closes the loop. Not here is how to do it well, but here is proof it was done well. The distinction looks small and is not: the first is content, infinitely reproducible and heading to zero. The second is infrastructure, has to be maintained, has to be trusted, and someone has to stand behind it when it’s wrong.

The uncomfortable version of this: the durable business is no longer knowing things. It’s being accountable for them.
