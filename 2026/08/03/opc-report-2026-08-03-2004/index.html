Hallucinated CVEs Got Rated Critical. Provenance Is Now the Product.

Hallucinated CVEs Got Rated Critical. Provenance Is Now the Product.

Four separate systems failed the same way in 72 hours: they accepted AI-generated artifacts they could not verify. The gap between “produced” and “confirmed” is the largest unclaimed market in software right now.

Key Insights

The call this hour: AI output has started corrupting the shared infrastructure that everyone else builds on — and every one of those failures is a paid job waiting for someone to take it.

This morning JFrog published an audit of a batch of SQLite advisories filed by a brand-new GitHub account. NVD had already rated several of them CRITICAL. CISA’s ADP program concurred. Red Hat initially scored one at 10.0. Then JFrog actually checked: the vulnerable function did not exist in the version the advisory named, the proof-of-concept payloads triggered no crash under AddressSanitizer, and none of the entries appeared on SQLite’s own advisory page. They believe 50+ CVEs from that single repository are LLM slop.

Nothing about that story is unique to security. It’s the same failure shape as the AUR pushing a hard stop, the same shape as the highest-voted post on Hacker News today, and the same shape as an AI-written news site being used as political cover. Generation is free, so generation happened. Nobody funded the confirmation step.

Four numbers that change a decision:

  • 9.8 CRITICAL on a function that doesn’t exist. Six SQLite advisories carried CVSS scores from 7.5 to 9.8 through NVD before an independent lab reproduced them and found nothing. One was quietly downgraded from 10.0 to 7.6 mid-audit. Institutional review is no longer a filter you can outsource trust to.
  • AUR pushes fully suspended. Arch’s DevOps team disabled package adoption on July 30, then killed all pushes on August 1, because of an influx of malicious adoptions. This follows the June wave that poisoned 400+ then 1,500+ packages by editing only build scripts — harvesting browser cookies, SSH keys, GitHub credentials, and OpenAI/ChatGPT bearer tokens.
  • 809 points, 356 comments, one day. “Don’t be a meat proxy” — a 400-word complaint about people relaying raw model output into Slack and PRs — was the top story on Hacker News today. The most upvoted engineering opinion this week is that unverified AI output is a negative-value contribution.
  • 8,410 stars in 5 days. YC shipped qm, a multiplayer agent harness for whole companies: per-person sandboxes, scoped memory, keychain, crons, shared skills, admin-set security posture. 887 forks already. The agent layer just moved from personal tool to org infrastructure, and the reference implementation is free.

Market Trends

1. Provenance failure has jumped from annoying to load-bearing

For eighteen months “AI slop” meant bad blog posts. This week it means a national vulnerability database issuing a critical rating for a bug that was never real.

The mechanics are worth understanding because they generalize. The attacker — or careless enthusiast, the JFrog post doesn’t assert intent — needed no exploit. They needed a plausible-looking advisory. The entire downstream chain is optimized for volume, not verification: NVD ingests, ADP enriches, scanners consume, dashboards go red, and somewhere a solo developer spends a Saturday patching a phantom.

JFrog’s method is the tell. To disprove six advisories they had to clone the repo at three tags, compile in isolated Docker containers, run every PoC verbatim under ASan, and audit CPE metadata across NVD and GHSA feeds. That is a full afternoon of skilled engineering per claim. Cost to generate: seconds. Cost to refute: hours. When that ratio inverts, the defending side loses by default — unless someone industrializes the refutation.

What this means for you: every institution that currently accepts an artifact on presentation quality is now exposed. Security advisories today. Grant applications, insurance claims, expert testimony, academic submissions, and code review are all built on the same broken assumption.

2. Package ecosystems are the softest target, and they know it

Arch shutting down all AUR pushes is not a minor operational hiccup — it’s a maintainer team admitting the review model doesn’t hold. And the June attack pattern is the one to internalize: package names unchanged, history unchanged, only the build script modified. Nothing looked different. The payload ran at build time and walked off with SSH keys and API bearer tokens.

Note what was on that loot list: OpenAI/ChatGPT tokens. Developer credentials are now a portfolio, and AI API keys are the newest liquid asset in it. If you are a one-person company, your laptop holds your infrastructure keys, your model credentials, your customers’ data access, and your deploy pipeline — with no security team standing between a poisoned build script and all of it.

The uncomfortable part: AUR is a volunteer-run ecosystem that could pause. npm, PyPI, VS Code extensions, and MCP server registries are commercial, always-on, and growing faster. They cannot pause.

3. “Reading AI output” is now recognized as a cost, not a benefit

Two of today’s most-discussed posts say the same thing from opposite ends. Gruhn’s “meat proxy” argues that relaying model output is actively subtractive: “I can talk to Claude myself. It’s going to be faster and I get to control the context.” His example of the problem is a real sentence he received — “NATS control-plane events: stream leader election / R3 quorum re-form during pod churn” — jargon dense enough that he had to look up nearly every word.

Ankur Sethi arrives from the builder’s side. He asks his coding assistant to output code in chat only, then retypes every line by hand into the editor. His own accounting: instead of 10x faster, he’s roughly 2x faster — and he keeps a working mental model of his own codebase. He calls the alternative cognitive debt.

Put next to Stack Overflow’s observation that developers bond to tools because tools encode trust, the picture is coherent: the market has finished the “wow, it generates” phase and entered the “who is accountable for this” phase. Verbosity is now a defect. Confidence without provenance is now a liability.

4. The agent layer went org-scale, and the free reference implementation just shipped

While trust was breaking, distribution shifted. YC’s qm hit 8,410 stars and 887 forks in five days with a deliberately different design premise: most agents are personal assistants, and forcing one to serve a whole company gets complex fast. So qm gives every employee an isolated workspace — own memory, files, keychain view, permissions, crons, sandbox — plus shared channels and projects, with admin-level control over security posture and which models are allowed. It runs Pi, OpenCode, Codex, or Claude Code against the same core, so no vendor is welded in.

Alongside it, trycompai/crm — an “agentic-first” open-source CRM — took 2,238 stars in three days.

For a solopreneur this is a two-sided signal. The downside: “I’ll build an agent wrapper for teams” just got its floor removed — the harness is free and backed by YC. The upside is bigger. Free infrastructure creates a deployment gap, and deployment gaps are where one-person consultancies make their best money. Somebody has to actually stand this up for a 30-person company, wire the permissions, write the skill packs, and set the security posture. That somebody is not going to be YC.

Build Directions

Ordered by time-to-first-dollar. Each includes what to build, the stack, and rough cost/timeline.

1. Claim reproduction as a service — start with security advisories

What: Industrialize exactly what JFrog did by hand. Take an incoming vulnerability claim, automatically check out the named version, build in an isolated container, execute the PoC under sanitizers, diff the cited symbols against the actual source tree, and emit a verdict: reproduced / not reproduced / inconclusive, with artifacts attached. You are not detecting AI. You are executing the claim. That distinction is the entire moat — detectors are contestable, reproduction is not.

Layer Choice Notes
Sandbox Docker + gVisor, hard network egress rules PoCs are hostile by definition
Build matrix Nix or per-language toolchain images Must hit exact tags cheaply
Instrumentation AddressSanitizer / Valgrind / libFuzzer The deterministic oracle
Symbol audit tree-sitter + git log -S Catch “function didn’t exist yet” instantly
Triage LLM Self-hosted 27B via vLLM for bulk parse; frontier model only on disputes Cost control
Output Signed report + JSON verdict + webhook Sell the artifact, not the dashboard

Cost & timeline: 4–6 weeks to a working pipeline for one language family (start with C/C++ — best sanitizer tooling, and where the SQLite mess happened). Price per claim, not per seat, and target buyers who eat noise for a living: managed security providers, OSS foundations, vulnerability scanner vendors who are quietly embarrassed by false criticals, and cyber insurers. Do not try to cover every ecosystem — depth in one language beats breadth across ten.

2. Build-script change watchdog for the ecosystems that can’t pause

What: The AUR attack changed only build scripts. So watch build scripts. Monitor a developer’s or a company’s actual dependency set across AUR, npm (postinstall), PyPI (setup.py), VS Code extensions, and — increasingly important — MCP server manifests. Alert on maintainer change, ownership transfer, new network egress in a build step, new credential-path reads, or a base64 blob appearing where there wasn’t one. You’re selling “tell me the moment someone else’s package starts behaving differently.”

Layer Choice
Ingest Registry webhooks + polling; git diff on AUR PKGBUILDs
Analysis tree-sitter AST diff, not text diff — defeats formatting noise
Heuristics Egress calls, ~/.ssh, ~/.aws, env-var sweeps, encoded payloads
Delivery Slack/Discord/email + a pre-install CI gate
Backend Supabase + a worker queue; boring on purpose

Cost & timeline: 3–4 weeks to MVP. Start free for individuals to build the corpus, charge teams $30–100/seat/month. Fastest credibility path: publish the diffs you catch publicly. In the trust business, a public track record is the marketing. Real risk: registries may ship this natively — so aim at the cross-registry view, which no single registry will ever build.

3. Deployment partner for org-scale agent harnesses

What: qm is free, capable, and five days old. Every 20–50 person company that reads about it will want it and will not be able to run it. Become the person who does. Fixed-scope engagement: deploy the core, wire Slack and identity, set up per-person sandboxes and keychain scoping, write the first five org skill packs against their actual systems, define the security posture, hand over a runbook. Then charge a monthly retainer for skill maintenance — because their systems change and the skills rot.

Layer Choice
Harness qm (or OpenCode / Claude Code depending on client policy)
Data plane Postgres — it’s the harness’s own dependency, keep it simple
Model policy LiteLLM gateway so the client can switch providers without you
Isolation Per-scope sandboxes; treat keychain scoping as the deliverable
Handover Runbook + recorded walkthrough — reduces support load enormously

Cost & timeline: First client inside 2 weeks if you already know one. Near-zero capital, highest hourly rate on this list. Price the deployment at $8–20k and the retainer at $1.5–4k/month. Warning: a five-day-old repo will break under you. Contract for a support window, not perpetual free fixes, and never promise uptime on someone else’s alpha.

4. Decision-output tooling — products that shrink reading

What: Today’s top HN post is a market brief. Anything that increases the volume a human must read is now net-negative, and everyone feels it. Build tools whose output is a verdict plus its evidence, never prose. Concretely: a PR reviewer that emits blocking / non-blocking / question with the three lines that matter and a reproduction command — not three paragraphs of hedged commentary. Same pattern applies to ticket triage, contract review, and monitoring alerts.

Implementation notes: enforce a hard token ceiling on user-visible output and let the model be verbose only internally. Add a “show your work” fold so the long version exists but nobody is forced through it. Ship a GitHub App + Next.js dashboard; use a self-hosted 27B for the bulk pass and reserve a frontier model for the final judgment call. The measurable promise is “output shorter than input” — put it on the pricing page as a guarantee.

Cost & timeline: 4 weeks. Hardest part isn’t technical, it’s discipline — every instinct and every model default pushes toward more words. Your product is the restraint.

5. Credential blast-radius audit for one-person companies

What: The AUR payload took SSH keys, GitHub credentials, and AI API tokens off developer machines. Most solo founders have no idea what’s currently on theirs. Sell a one-shot audit plus remediation: enumerate every long-lived credential on the machine, map what each one can reach, replace static keys with short-lived tokens, move model keys behind a gateway, and set up spend alarms that catch a stolen key within hours instead of at the invoice. You’re selling a bounded, finishable job with a clear before/after — which is exactly what sells to people who hate security projects.

Implementation notes: gitleaks and trufflehog for the sweep, 1Password/Infisical for storage, LiteLLM as the model-key chokepoint with per-key budget caps, cloud-native short-lived tokens wherever available. Deliver a one-page blast-radius map. Charge $1–3k flat, deliver in three days, and offer a quarterly re-check. This is the fastest cash on this list and it doubles as lead generation for Direction 2.

Risk Factors

  • AI detectors are not evidence, and building on them is a lawsuit. JFrog used GPTZero as one signal and then proved the case by execution. If your product’s verdict rests on a detector score, you will eventually flag a real human’s real work and there is no recovery from that. Reproduce, don’t classify.
  • Reproduction doesn’t scale linearly, and your pricing must admit it. Some claims resolve in ninety seconds; some need an afternoon of a senior engineer. Flat-rate pricing on unbounded work is the classic way this business dies. Tier it, cap it, and charge separately for the hard ones.
  • You are one false negative from zero. Trust businesses have asymmetric downside: a hundred correct verdicts buy you nothing, one missed real vulnerability ends the company. Say “inconclusive” loudly and often — it is a feature, not a weakness.
  • Ecosystem-dependent products die when the ecosystem patches. AUR will fix this. npm will tighten postinstall. If your entire product is one registry’s current weakness, you have a quarter, not a business. Build the cross-registry view that no single registry has an incentive to ship.
  • Free org-scale harnesses cut both ways. qm deletes the “team agent platform” startup category and simultaneously creates the deployment services market. If your plan was to sell the platform, you are now late. If it was to sell the outcome, you just got a free engine.
  • Volume is going to get worse before it gets better. FIRST’s own 2026 forecast centers around 59,000 CVE disclosures, with the upper end of its confidence range near 118,000. Human triage is not going to catch up. That’s the tailwind under this whole thesis — and also the reason your throughput assumptions will be wrong in six months.

Action Recommendations

If you’re starting out

  • ✅ Run Direction 5 on yourself first, this week. Audit your own credential blast radius before selling the service. You will find something, and that finding becomes your first sales conversation.
  • ✅ Pick one language family and go deep on reproduction. C/C++ has the best sanitizer story and the freshest scandal. Depth in one ecosystem beats a shallow multi-language demo every time.
  • ✅ Publish every verdict you produce. In the trust business, the public track record is the entire funnel. One well-documented refutation on Hacker News outperforms three months of cold email.
  • ✅ Stop relaying model output to clients. Gruhn’s post is a customer telling you exactly what they hate. Read it, verify it, then write in your own words — that’s the value you’re actually billing for.

If you already have a product or a small team

  • ✅ Audit what your build pipeline trusts, today. List every dependency whose maintainer could change without you noticing. The AUR attack modified nothing but build scripts and nobody spotted it visually.
  • ✅ Put every model key behind a gateway with a hard spend cap. Bearer tokens are now on the standard credential-theft loot list. A cap turns a catastrophe into an incident report.
  • ✅ Measure output length against input length on your AI features. If your product makes users read more than they wrote, you’re on the wrong side of today’s clearest market signal.
  • ✅ Decide this week whether you’re on the platform side or the outcome side. qm at 8,410 stars in five days settles the platform question. Reposition toward outcomes before the free tier reaches your customers.

What It Means

Yesterday’s frontier story was about capability getting cheap. Today’s is about what breaks when it does. Four independent systems — a vulnerability database, a package registry, a code review culture, and a news outlet — all failed the identical way inside 72 hours: they accepted artifacts they had no cheap way to confirm.

That asymmetry is the whole opportunity. Producing a claim costs seconds. Confirming it costs hours. Every institution that built its process on “if it looks credible, it probably is” is now running an unpatched vulnerability in its own workflow, and most of them don’t have anyone assigned to fix it.

So the play isn’t to generate more. Everyone can generate. The play is to be the one who can prove — cheaply, repeatedly, and with artifacts attached. Capability is commodity by Christmas. Confirmation is a business.
