Incorporation Is Now an API Call, and Humans Approving Agent Commands Miss One Threat in Three

Incorporation Is Now an API Call, and Humans Approving Agent Commands Miss One Threat in Three

August 7, 2026

Two things happened in the same twenty-four hours, and they only make sense together.

The first: Naïve raised $28.5 million in a Series A led by Nexus Venture Partners, with YC participating. What it sells is infrastructure that lets your AI agents do the work of running a business — U.S. LLC formation, email inboxes, virtual cards, phone numbers, databases, compute, Stripe and QuickBooks connections — all behind one API you hand to Cursor, Claude Code, or Codex as a prompt. You show up for KYC/KYB and to pay. Ten full-time employees. Over 30,000 developer customers within months of launch. Annual run-rate revenue up 10x in six months.

The second: a developer published statistics from a browser game where you play human-in-the-loop for a coding agent, approving or denying its commands under time pressure. The sample is 40,000 sessions and 409,000 individual approve/deny decisions. The average player caught 66.3% of threats. One in three got through.

Put those side by side and the shape of the year changes. Can one person run a company is now an infrastructure question, and infrastructure questions get answered by vendors. The question that’s left — the one nobody has a good product for yet — is how big your blast radius is when the agent gets it wrong.

Key Insights

  • The company layer got commoditized. Naïve packages incorporation, payments, phone numbers, email, cloud and accounting integrations behind a single API that an agent orchestrates. Ten employees, 30,000+ developer customers, 10x ARR growth in six months, $28.5M Series A, ~$32M raised total.
  • The fastest-growing customer segment is AI automation agencies. Per CEO Sean Dorje: “the first business that a lot of people start is genuinely just selling agents to other small businesses.” Also on the platform: faceless TikTok/YouTube channels and at least one autonomously operated rental car agency.
  • Inference cost is now the top line item for autonomous businesses, in the vendor’s own words — and it’s the fastest-growing source of demand for Naïve, ahead of the incorporation toolkit itself. They’re building a model router, a memory layer, an orchestrator, and a serverless agent runtime on lightweight JS environments so you pay when an agent is active instead of renting a VM per agent.
  • The human-in-the-loop is not a security control. Mean accuracy 66.3% across 409,000 decisions. 32.9% of sessions ended net negative — penalties from approved threats plus blocked safe commands outweighed everything done right. 7% approved literally everything.
  • Quiet attacks get through three times more often than loud ones. Obvious destruction (rm -rf /) missed 11.7% of the time. Credential exfiltration missed 33.4%. Scope violations like cat ~/.aws/credentials missed 35.0%.
  • The single most-missed command was npm run analyze at 64.7% — approved even though the history log directly above the prompt showed the script piping build output to an unknown endpoint. Pooled across three npm run variants the miss rate was 52.5% versus 28.4% for other exfiltration-style attacks. Hiding a payload behind a familiar script name roughly doubles its success rate.
  • AI search is a net gain for the long tail, not a tax. Shopify’s Q2 call: AI-driven traffic and orders tripled year over year, described as “a complement to search, rather than a substitute,” with the benefit concentrated in smaller merchants. The opposite of what happened to publishers.
  • The harness layer is racing to zero. Meta shipped Muse Code (terminal agent, fans out to parallel sub-agents in isolated worktrees). Prime Intellect open-sourced Prime Agent (IPython REPL as the model’s only tool; sub-agents are function calls; the agent can CRUD its own prompts, skills, memory and sub-agents). Herdr went to YC with a free runtime.

Market Trends

1. The company layer is commoditized, and scarcity moved downstream

For two years the one-person-company narrative was about making the thing: AI writes your code, your copy, your design. Naïve’s round marks a more complete shift — everything that isn’t the product also became an API call.

Read the customer list carefully, because it tells you where the money actually is. The fastest-growing segment isn’t people building novel products; it’s AI automation agencies — people selling agents to small businesses that don’t want to hire. Behind that: faceless content channels, and a rental car agency running autonomously.

The more important detail is buried in what Naïve is spending the money on. Not more incorporation features. Four infrastructure projects: virtualized agent sandboxes, model routing and inference optimization, a memory layer, and governance/orchestration. Dorje’s framing: “Part of running an autonomous company and running agents, like that’s your biggest cost line now.”

That is a vendor telling you, on the record, that the constraint on autonomous business is not capability. It’s the bill and the blast radius.

What this means for you: if your differentiation is “I can set up and run a company alone,” that differentiation has a shelf life measured in months, because it’s now a purchase. What survives is domain knowledge narrow enough that a general agent can’t fake it, and unit economics that hold when inference isn’t subsidized.

2. Human approval is a liability-transfer mechanism, not a safety mechanism

This is the hardest data of the week and it deserves reading in full.

Threat category Examples Miss rate
Obvious destructive rm -rf /, chmod -R 777 / 11.7%
Persistent mutation crontab injection, git config hijack 23.8%
Exfiltration / code execution curl to unknown APIs, typosquatted packages 33.4%
Scope violations cat ~/.aws/credentials, cat ~/.kube/config 35.0%

The commands that actually steal your credentials are missed three times as often as the ones that just look scary.

And the standout finding: npm run analyze was approved 64.7% of the time, the single most-missed command in the dataset — even though the agent’s own history log, displayed directly above the approval prompt, showed the script definition:

Two thirds approved it anyway. Across the three npm run variants in the game, pooled miss rate was 52.5% (n=9,482) against 28.4% for every other exfiltration-style attack. Familiar wrapper, double the success rate, payload in plain sight.

There’s a structural point underneath, and a commenter on the thread put it better than the post did: approving npm run build means approving an arbitrary shell command written in package.json — and editing package.json is something the agent does without asking. You are reviewing commands while the threat lives in files.

The other half of the dilemma is over-blocking. Benign commands routinely denied: npm config set registry https://npm.internal at 59%, rm -rf dist/ at 45%, kill $(lsof -t -i:3000) at 43%. That noise is what produces the 7% who eventually approve everything. The miss-rate curve within a session confirms it — accuracy improves for the first few prompts, then degrades toward the end.

The sharpest comment in the thread: “The goal of human-in-the-loop is to have someone liable for potential damages, rather than to prevent disasters.”

What this means for you: stop budgeting attention as a control. Budget isolation. Sandboxes, short-lived credentials issued on demand, egress allowlists, tool-call proxies. Give the agent capabilities, not a shell.

3. AI search is redistributing distribution toward the long tail

Shopify’s Q2 earnings call produced a number that runs directly counter to the publishing industry’s experience: AI-driven traffic and orders tripled year over year, and President Harley Finkelstein characterized AI as “a complement to search, rather than a substitute for it,” with the long tail of e-commerce — the small merchants who make up most of Shopify’s base — benefiting most.

Same week, Google Maps added agentic food ordering and hotel booking, turning a navigation product into a transaction surface. And in the indie communities, tools that check whether ChatGPT, Claude, and Perplexity recommend your business are starting to appear.

The direction is legible: the next SEO fight is about being named inside a model’s answer. That fight is structurally friendlier to small operators than classic SEO was, because it rewards clean structured facts and verifiable first-hand specificity more than it rewards domain authority accumulated over a decade.

4. Harnesses are converging to free — don’t build your identity there

Three moves in one week. Meta shipped Muse Code in beta: a terminal coding agent on its Muse Spark model that, per Zuckerberg, “fans out to separate sub-agents working in parallel in isolated worktrees” when a job is big enough, without touching your working copy. Prime Intellect open-sourced Prime Agent, architecturally the most interesting of the three: a persistent IPython REPL is the model’s only tool, sub-agents are function calls inside it, and the harness treats its own prompts, skills, memory, and sub-agents as things the agent can create, read, update, and delete mid-trajectory. Herdr took its terminal runtime into YC and kept it free.

The comment section on the Herdr post is the useful part: “Enshitification is 100% guaranteed with any vc backing. Maybe not now, but definitely in the future — it is the literal point of venture capital. Gain adoption, then exploit to the maximum extent possible. It’s fine to use these kind of tools, just don’t get too attached and be ready for the rug pull.”

There’s also a practical objection worth sitting with, from a developer in the same thread: “I find myself to be a huge bottleneck, having to do technical design and design reviews… I feel like there’s not enough coding work to justify multiple agents.” Parallel agent orchestration is being sold ahead of the demand for it.

What this means for you: free open runtimes are distribution you rent, not foundation you own. Keep your switching cost at the level of a config change.

Build Directions

1. Agent blast-radius control (the strongest opening this week)

What to build: not another approval dialog — the data says approval UI is a dead end. Build the layer that makes dangerous actions physically unavailable. Per-session sandboxes. Credentials issued short-lived by a broker instead of sitting in ~/.aws and shell profiles. Egress through an allowlisted proxy with an audit log. Tool calls through an MCP proxy that trims capabilities per session.

Your pitch writes itself from three numbers: 35.0% miss rate on scope violations, 33.4% on exfiltration, 52.5% on indirect execution via npm run.

Stack: Docker + gVisor or Firecracker for isolation, Vault or Doppler for short-lived credentials, a self-built MCP proxy for tool-call interception and logging, an egress allowlist. The deliverable customers actually pay for is the audit report.

Cost/timeline: 4–8 weeks to something demoable. Distribution is close to free — publish your own scan results as a public leaderboard; those numbers are inherently newsworthy.

2. Vertical automation agency on top of company-as-an-API

What to build: Naïve says AI automation agencies are its fastest-growing segment. That’s both the opportunity and the warning — the lane is filling. The differentiator is not your stack, it’s knowing one industry’s actual workflow: dental appointment-and-insurance reconciliation, freight forwarding documentation, records handling for a regional law practice. Package the domain knowledge as skills and evals. Outsource the company.

Stack: Claude Code or Codex to build, Naïve-style infrastructure for incorporation/payments/numbers/inboxes, Stripe for revenue, Postgres/Supabase for state. Turn on the governance layer’s budget caps and human approval for sensitive actions — that’s not optional when the agent has a corporate card.

Cost/timeline: 4–8 weeks to a first paying customer if you already know the industry. If you don’t, skip this one; the domain knowledge is the margin.

3. AI search visibility (AEO) for long-tail merchants

What to build: Shopify’s numbers prove AI traffic is net-positive for small merchants, and almost none of those merchants know where they stand inside a model’s answer. Ship continuous monitoring: run a fixed question set against the major assistants on a schedule, record whether your client is mentioned, how they’re described, and who gets named instead, then output the structured-data and page fixes.

Stack: schema.org structured data and crawlable product pages as the deliverable, scheduled runs against ChatGPT/Perplexity/Gemini, Postgres for the trend history, a weekly email report.

Cost/timeline: 2–4 weeks to MVP, $49–199/month is painless for a small merchant. Note this is a workload made for cheap models and off-peak batching — build it that way from day one.

4. Verification as the deliverable

What to build: writing code is free; confirming the code didn’t do something else is not. The npm run finding generalizes: indirection is the blind spot in every review process, human or automated. Sell adversarial review to small teams shipping fast with AI and nobody watching — auto-generated tests against new code, scans of build scripts and dependency changes, and a filed risk opinion on every agent-authored diff.

Stack: Claude Code sub-agents as adversarial reviewers, property-based testing to fill coverage, a CI gate to make it binding, and a report the customer can forward to their own client.

Cost/timeline: 3–6 weeks. The structural advantage: this category’s value rises as code generation gets cheaper, rather than falling.

5. Skills and evals on top of someone else’s open harness

What to build: Prime Agent is open source, Muse Code is a free beta, Herdr’s runtime stays free. Runtime pricing is going to zero — don’t ship the eighth one. The scarce artifact is the skill pack and eval suite that gets a general agent to deliverable quality in one specific profession: the prompts, the tool wrappers, the acceptance criteria.

Stack: Prime Agent (open source, REPL architecture, sub-agent CRUD and cross-session agent-to-agent messaging) or Herdr’s plugin surface as the base. You own the skills and the evals; they own the runtime.

Cost/timeline: days to weeks. The tradeoff is platform risk — price it in, don’t ignore it.

Risk Factors

  • Don’t count human approval as a control. 66.3% mean accuracy means you wave through roughly one in three threats, and it degrades as the session runs long. 7% of players approved everything. Your eyes are not the safeguard; the sandbox is.
  • Over-blocking is its own failure mode. Benign commands denied 43–59% of the time generate the noise that produces reflexive approval. The vigilance and the fatigue are the same phenomenon at different points on the curve.
  • Inference is the top cost line for autonomous operations, per the company selling the infrastructure. Hardware relief is further out than the headlines suggest: AMD’s acquisition of Taalas — which etches models directly into silicon — is real, but the public demo runs a Llama-8B-class model, and commenters noted memory bandwidth remains the bottleneck regardless. Don’t model this year’s margin on next year’s silicon.
  • Vendor concentration is quantifiable and it isn’t just a small-company problem. Analysis of Microsoft filings suggests roughly 70% of its AI revenue is concentrated on OpenAI. If your build, deploy, and model inference all route through one vendor, that’s one failure, not three.
  • Free VC-backed runtimes will change. Adoption first, extraction later is the model. Keep your migration cost at config-change level.
  • The floor dropping means the crowd arrives faster. When incorporation is an API call, it’s an API call for your competitors too. The moat has to be domain knowledge, customer relationships, and a name attached to a track record.

Action Recommendations

If you’re early or solo:

  • ✅ Move your agent into a sandbox this week and pull API keys out of your shell profile into a separately sourced secrets file. Highest return-on-effort item on this list; costs an afternoon.
  • ✅ Audit who can modify package.json and your build scripts, and whether a change triggers an alert. That’s the highest-miss-rate entry point in the dataset by roughly 2x.
  • ✅ Don’t ship approval UI #8 or session-manager #9. Ship isolation, or ship into an industry you actually understand.
  • ✅ If your customers are e-commerce or local businesses, run the assistant-mention test on them this week. It’s an unsaturated distribution window and the earnings data now backs it.
  • ✅ Outsource incorporation, payments, numbers, and inboxes to an API. Spend the reclaimed time on domain depth and customer relationships — the only assets the next API update can’t flatten.

If you’re running something established:

  • ✅ Re-run unit economics at +50% inference cost and decide now which features get cheaper models and which get cut.
  • ✅ Audit your agents’ real permission boundary with three questions: can it edit build scripts? can it read long-lived credentials? can it reach arbitrary domains? Three yeses means your exposure is unbounded.
  • ✅ Stand up a second deployment path that doesn’t depend on your primary CI vendor, and actually run it once.
  • ✅ Push batchable inference to off-peak and move mechanical calls — extraction, classification, formatting — to the cheapest tier that passes your evals.
  • ✅ Re-examine your distribution assumptions. If your traffic model still assumes people arrive via search, go look at your AI-referred order share. That number tripled year over year for Shopify merchants in Q2.

What It Means

The bottleneck moved twice in two years. It used to be can you build it. Then it was can you get anyone to see it. As of this week, with incorporation, payments, deployment, and phone numbers all reduced to an API call, it’s can you let it run without blowing yourself up.

And the data already answered the obvious first attempt at that. Watching every command yourself misses a third of threats, and the longer you watch the worse you get. So both questions this report exists to answer — which direction, and with which tools — resolve to the same place: build toward verification and isolation. Replace your attention with sandboxes, short-lived credentials, and tool proxies. Rent the company infrastructure. Spend everything you save on the judgment nobody can provision through an API.

The one-person company still works. It just stopped being a question of whether you can build it, and became a question of whether you can trust it to run.
