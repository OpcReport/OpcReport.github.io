The Company That Invented Forward-Deployed Engineers Just Posted 93% Growth. A Startup Selling Escape From Them Raised $20M.

The Company That Invented Forward-Deployed Engineers Just Posted 93% Growth. A Startup Selling Escape From Them Raised $20M.

August 4, 2026 — trend monitoring for independent builders and one-person companies

Key Insights

The headline judgment: What is being funded right now is not “build an agent.” It is “make an agent work inside a decade of accumulated technical debt.” June came out of stealth this week with a $20M pre-seed raised without a pitch deck, on the explicit thesis that AI has increased demand for professional services rather than eliminated it. On the same day, Palantir — the company that popularized the forward-deployed engineer role — reported 93% year-over-year revenue growth and raised full-year guidance by roughly $5B. Those are two sides of one trade. For a one-person company there is a product spec buried in it, and a customer said it out loud: people are sick of consultants and black boxes. You cannot sell a model. You can sell the fact that you know exactly how filthy the data is in one specific industry.

Start with June, because the founder’s framing is the cleanest statement of the opportunity anyone has made this quarter. Efrat Rapoport, a former Salesforce executive, put it this way: “Paradoxically, AI has increased the need for professional services. The industry’s answer to AI deployment is: hire, hire, and hire more.”

Rapoport and co-founders Ohad Hen, Barak Goldstein, and Idan Tsitiat previously built Bonobo AI — a pre-Transformer language model company that shipped speech-to-text in 2017 and was acquired by Salesforce in 2019. They spent the following years inside Salesforce watching customers hit the same wall repeatedly: the model was never the problem, integrating it was. The pre-seed was led by Marc Benioff’s Time Ventures, with Michael Dell, Aaron Levie, and George Kurtz participating. Valuation undisclosed. “We didn’t even prepare a deck for this round,” Rapoport said.

The wall they are attacking is unglamorous and completely real. Despite persistent “death of SaaS” talk, nobody is vibe-coding a CRM for a Fortune 500 company. An AI model dropped into an enterprise still has to cooperate with Salesforce, ServiceNow, Databricks, Workday, and dozens of other data platforms. “Before AI can create value, someone has to deal with the legacy systems first,” Rapoport said. “Data is scattered across platforms, workflows are tangled, and years of technical debt have piled up.” Building an agent template is the easy part. The hard part: “When there are 10 duplicate fields in the database that all mean the same thing, and different teams each use a different subset, how is the agent supposed to know what to do?”

June’s product is that insight rendered literally: scan the customer’s existing systems, map business processes, locate bottlenecks, then generate a step-by-step deployment roadmap — “delete these duplicate fields, connect this data source” — with a Build button on each task, and automatic notifications to the relevant teams through internal comms.

Now the part that should go straight into your product notes. Paul Akinmade, Chief Strategy Officer at CMG, a large US mortgage lender, had moved software engineering onto Claude Code and then got stuck integrating with Salesforce. He had publicly committed at the previous year’s Salesforce annual conference to come back with 100 agents in production, and that promise was slipping. His team spent weeks meeting architects, consulting forward-deployed engineers, asking anyone who would talk — and got nowhere. When he first considered trialing June, he told Rapoport directly:

“If your product requires an FDE, I don’t want it. I’ve been through that and I’m sick of it. I don’t want a black box, I don’t want something only certain people can understand. I want a simple, usable tool.”

Rapoport positions June as a complement to FDEs and consultants. Her customers may be buying it for the opposite reason: it lets them bypass FDEs entirely. That quote is a free PRD, and it names a category with budget already attached.

Four numbers that should change your decisions:

  • $20M pre-seed, zero slides. The problem being funded is duplicate database fields and tangled workflows, not model capability. The entry ticket to this category is domain knowledge, not compute.
  • $1.94B revenue, +93% YoY, guidance raised from $7.65–7.66B to $8.15–8.16B. Palantir Q2: adjusted EPS $0.41 vs $0.35 expected, net income $1.07B against roughly $329M a year earlier, stock up nearly 15% after hours. CEO Alex Karp said the strong growth “looks like it can continue for at least another 18 months.” Deployment is, verifiably, the most profitable business in enterprise AI right now.
  • 88% of exploits land within 48 hours. CrowdStrike’s 2026 Threat Hunting Report has changed the unit of measurement for the exploitation window from days to hours. Your patch cadence is now a commercial risk, not an ops preference.
  • 87% of registry threats are malicious npm packages. One group compromised 300+ dependencies in a single day; another injected malicious dependencies into at least 131 Mastra AI framework packages. A solo founder shipping high-frequency agent commits into an automated build pipeline is the ideal target profile.

Market Trends

1. AI did not kill professional services. It turned them into the bottleneck.

The prevailing 2025 narrative was that AI would collapse the services layer — that if a model can write the integration, you do not need the integrator. What actually happened is that the integration surface grew faster than the automation of it. Every new agent needs to touch more systems, respect more permissions, and reconcile more conflicting representations of the same entity than the workflow it replaced.

This is why the FDE role went from a Palantir curiosity to an industry-wide job title. And it is worth noting how precisely the deliverables have now been specified. Anthropic’s forward-deployed engineer role describes building production applications with Claude inside customer systems and delivering artifacts such as MCP servers, sub-agents, and agent skills, then codifying repeatable deployment patterns back into the product.

Read that as a market signal rather than a job ad. When an industry can enumerate its deliverables, those deliverables can be productized — and productizing is the one structural advantage an independent builder has over a consulting firm. You cannot hire an FDE bench. You do not need to. You need to turn one deployment pattern in one industry into something that installs itself.

The competency framing that has emerged around this role is instructive too. The useful version of an FDE is not “engineer who talks to customers.” It is someone who can enter a messy environment, find the high-value operational problem behind the stated request, map the workflow and the data, build a production-grade first version, set governance boundaries, measure business impact, and turn the whole thing into a reusable pattern. Notice how much of that is judgment and how little of it is throughput. Judgment does not scale by hiring, which is exactly why it is available to you.

2. Palantir’s quarter is the public-market proof that deployment is the product

Palantir reported Q2 revenue of $1.94B, up 93% year over year against expectations of $1.81B. Adjusted EPS came in at $0.41 versus $0.35 expected. Net income was $1.07B, against roughly $329M in the year-ago quarter. Full-year revenue guidance moved from $7.65–7.66B to $8.15–8.16B, and full-year adjusted operating income guidance to $4.89–4.90B against analyst expectations of $4.51B. The stock rose nearly 15% after hours.

The interesting thing is not the number. It is the pairing. The company that invented the forward-deployed engineering model just had its best quarter ever, on the same day a company selling escape from forward-deployed engineers raised a pre-seed from Benioff, Dell, and Levie. Both are selling deployment. One sells it as people, the other as product. The demand underneath is identical.

For a one-person company, only one of those two paths exists. You will never staff an FDE bench, so productization is not a strategic choice, it is the only door. And the barrier to walking through it is not engineering capability — it is whether you genuinely know what the data looks like in a specific industry, which fields are historical baggage, and which step in the process has a human quietly patching things in Excel. That knowledge is not in any API doc, and it does not depreciate when the next model ships.

3. Your deployment surface is your attack surface, and it is already bleeding

CrowdStrike published its 2026 Threat Hunting Report on August 3, based on OverWatch team observations and twelve months of tracking 290+ named threat groups through June 30. This year’s edition includes automated attacks in the counts for the first time. Several findings translate directly into how you should write your CI config tomorrow.

The exploitation window is now measured in hours. Between January and June 2026, 88% of cases showed a gap of 48 hours or less between public proof-of-concept and actual exploitation; zero-day exploitation rose 42% in the prior reporting year. The reference case: CVE-2025-55182, an unauthenticated remote code execution flaw in React Server Components and Next.js, disclosed alongside its patch on December 3, 2025. Working exploit code appeared the next day. Two groups attacked within 24 hours, and OverWatch tracked over 800 hunting leads against 80+ victims in the first four days.

AI infrastructure is being probed directly. Techniques targeting AI model access accounted for 16% of observed MITRE ATLAS techniques over the year. CrowdStrike’s honeypot infrastructure captured a payload containing a malicious MCP server configuration designed to read the parent process’s environment variables and exfiltrate configuration data to an external webhook. If you have shipped an MCP server that inherits your shell environment, that sentence is about you.

“LLMjacking” is now a named category. In a May campaign against a cloud provider’s foundation model service, an attacker escalated a compromised identity to administrator, filled out the use-case form required to unlock model access, and then fired nearly 200,000 API requests in two minutes until rate limiting kicked in. Your model quota is an extractable asset.

Registries remain the shortest path in. In H1 2026, malicious npm packages made up 87% of identified malware registry threats. Stardust Chollima compromised the Axios npm package in March using stolen maintainer credentials, then in June injected malicious dependencies into at least 131 Mastra AI framework packages — the entry point was an employee approached on LinkedIn, pulled into a video call, and induced to click a link. Altered Spider’s malware self-propagates, steals maintainer credentials, and republishes infected packages autonomously; one May campaign compromised 300+ dependencies in a single day. In March the same group altered Git tags on trivy-action, the official GitHub Action for Aqua Security’s Trivy scanner, meaning any organization pulling an affected version in an automated build ran credential-stealing malware inside its own pipeline.

Identity is the new front door. Vishing intrusions in H1 2026 ran at twice the frequency of H2 2025, after a 134% rise from 2024 to 2025. Monthly device-code phishing attempts grew 15x over six months. In one incident, an attacker went from account takeover to completed data exfiltration in under five minutes.

And agents are now on both sides of the board. OverWatch reports that detection leads triggered by AI agents are currently running at 2.5x the volume of human-triggered leads. Overall intrusion activity grew only about 4%, down from 27% in last year’s report, which CrowdStrike attributes to attackers investing time in fewer but more sophisticated operations. Adam Meyers, who leads counter-adversary operations, framed the takeaway bluntly: organizations must protect AI with the same intensity they adopt it.

Translate that for a solo builder: shipping high-frequency agent commits into an automated build with permissive CI credentials assembles all three of an attacker’s favorite conditions — rapid change, weak human review, and broad pipeline permissions — in one stack.

4. Review is moving up a layer: from reading code to judging output

Hoplite (YC S26) launched on HN this cycle with cloud-deployed coding agents. Founders Bence and Ryan pivoted away from the idea they applied to YC with — AI for retail investing — for the honest reason that it was not a product they would use themselves. Their thesis about where development is heading in 6–12 months is worth quoting as a planning assumption: as models improve, developers will review less and less code and instead review product output — evaluating new user flows, visually verifying features look right, checking the API behaves, confirming the CLI works on Windows — and doing it while running hundreds of agents concurrently.

Their infrastructure choices double as a reference stack for anyone running agents at volume: a custom in-house agent harness rather than Codex or Claude Code (explicitly to avoid waiting on Anthropic and OpenAI), hosted on AWS with Temporal for durable workflows, Modal for sandboxes, and Planetscale for the database — chosen on the belief that agents are becoming tier-0 infrastructure and need reliability and security to match.

Running alongside that, Sean Goedecke’s “LLMs reward expertise” climbed to 583 points and 250 comments and is still rising. His argument: LLMs turn everyone into a generalist, which leads people to conclude there is no skill in using them. That is wrong — the most important skill in prompting is expertise in the domain you are prompting for. His illustration is Terence Tao’s ChatGPT conversation about the recently discovered counterexample to the Jacobian Conjecture. What Tao does differently:

  • His messages are very short and to the point; he responds to the gist, not point-by-point.
  • The model’s outputs are markedly more concise than usual, because by signalling expertise he shunts the model into “talking-to-mathematicians” mode rather than “explaining-to-amateurs” mode.
  • He pushes back without directly contradicting — “this looks more complex than I was hoping for.”
  • He makes the leaps himself and almost never takes the model’s advice about where to go next.

Goedecke’s conclusion is the one that matters here: for many tasks the human is the bottleneck, not the model, because the hard part is communicating exactly what kind of solution you want. The information is already in the model; extracting it takes a very smart human.

Put the four threads together — June’s duplicate fields, Palantir’s FDE quarter, Hoplite’s shift to reviewing outcomes, Goedecke’s expertise thesis — and they converge: the part you can delegate to a model is depreciating fast, and knowing what “correct” looks like is appreciating fast. Knowing what correct looks like does not require headcount.

Build Directions

1. The no-FDE deployment scanner for one vertical

What to build: pick an industry you have actually worked in — insurance claims, mortgage origination, freight forwarding, clinical trials, SaaS revenue reconciliation — and build a read-only tool that connects to the customer’s core systems and outputs three things: a field-level duplicate and conflict inventory, a workflow bottleneck map, and a step-by-step deployment roadmap. Do not build the agent. Build the map you need before an agent is safe to deploy.

Stack: read-only connectors (Salesforce / NetSuite / ServiceNow / Postgres) → field similarity and semantic clustering via local embeddings plus rules, no frontier model required → Claude or GPT to render the roadmap in plain language → output to Notion, Markdown, and a Slack digest. Deliberately skip the one-click Build button; that is June’s ground. Yours is the map that makes people confident enough to act.

Timeline: 4–7 weeks solo to a chargeable v1. Pricing: $2,000–8,000 per scan, or $500–1,500/month with quarterly rescans. You are pricing against the weeks of architect meetings the customer is currently wasting, not against a software feature.

2. Supply-chain gates for CI in the agent era

What to build: a CI guard aimed squarely at the paths in the CrowdStrike report, for small teams shipping high-frequency agent commits. New dependency requires human confirmation. Maintainer-change alerts. Git tag drift detection — the trivy-action compromise was a moved tag. Egress allowlist during build. A quarantine period before newly introduced packages can reach a production pipeline.

Stack: GitHub Action / GitLab CI plugin → npm provenance plus Sigstore/cosign signature verification → lockfile diffing with a freshness policy (Renovate configured for policy, not auto-merge) → build-container egress allowlist → Slack alerting.

Timeline: 6–9 weeks. Pricing: $49–199/month per repository. The buyer profile is unambiguous: anyone who has already been burned once, or who just received their first enterprise security questionnaire. This sells on fear, not efficiency, which means you skip the market-education phase entirely.

3. MCP secret-boundary audits

What to build: the honeypot payload that read parent-process environment variables and exfiltrated to a webhook tells you that most self-built MCP servers are running with fully inherited host environments. Build a static plus dynamic audit for MCP servers: enumerate which environment variables it can actually read, which outbound requests it can initiate, which exposed tools are irreversible operations, and the provenance of any third-party MCP configuration in use.

Stack: Node/Python static scan of MCP declarations → dynamic run in a sandbox recording syscalls and network activity (Modal or a local container both work) → generate a PDF report formatted to drop straight into a customer security questionnaire.

Timeline: 3–5 weeks to a report-shaped v1. Pricing: $500–2,500 per audit, or convert to a recurring CI check. Ship the report first, not the platform — a report bills immediately; a platform waits on market education.

4. Domain correctness suites — selling “what good looks like”

What to build: Hoplite says review is moving to product output. Goedecke says domain expertise is what extracts value from a model. Combine them: a correctness-judgment suite for one vertical. Not generic evals — a codified answer to “in this industry, what counts as acceptable output, and which errors are catastrophic.”

Stack: promptfoo or Braintrust as the harness → OpenRouter to fan the same suite across the small, cheap models your users actually run, not the frontier model on your own subscription → a hand-written catalogue of domain failure modes, which is the actual moat → nightly runs with alerting on regression.

Timeline: 3–6 weeks. Pricing: $99–499/month. The first move is writing the ten irreversible operations in your industry by hand. Nobody can lift that list off your landing page.

5. Identity and device hardening for one-person companies

What to build: vishing doubled, device-code phishing grew 15x, and one intrusion went from account takeover to exfiltration in under five minutes. Every one of those attacks assumes nobody is available to second-check the target. A one-person company has no IT department by definition. Build a hardening checklist plus an automated audit for solo operators: device-code login disabled, SaaS OAuth grant inventory, hardware keys enforced on maintainer accounts, and a written verification procedure for “someone just approached me on LinkedIn.”

Stack: admin APIs across major SaaS providers for grant inventory → a local CLI that produces a report → a copyable incident playbook. Start as content and templates, validate that people pay, then engineer it.

Timeline: 2–4 weeks. Pricing: $19–79/month, or a $199 one-time hardening pack.

Risk Factors

  • Patch cadence is now a commercial risk. With 88% of exploits landing inside 48 hours, “I’ll update dependencies over the weekend” is an exposure — especially when your CI holds deploy credentials.
  • Your model quota is an extractable asset. The LLMjacking case ran nearly 200,000 requests in two minutes. If your key lives inside an MCP server that inherited the host environment, you will find out from your invoice before you find out from your logs.
  • Palantir’s 93% is both signal and warning. It confirms deployment is where the money is, and it confirms that the best-resourced players are accelerating into it. Pick industries and price points they will never touch; a head-on collision has no upside for you.
  • Inference pricing still rests on debt subsidy. Roughly $1.65T in off-balance-sheet hyperscaler borrowing underwrites current token economics. Stress-test your unit economics at 2–3x today’s inference cost and fix the model now if it does not survive.

Action Recommendations

If you are early (no revenue, or one small product):

  • ✅ Write down the ten irreversible operations in the industry you actually worked in. That list seeds directions 1 and 4 and cannot be copied off your landing page.
  • ✅ Check today which environment variables your MCP server or agent runtime can actually read. Ten minutes of work, and probably your single largest exposure right now.
  • ✅ Add human confirmation for new dependencies in CI. The faster your agents commit, the more that gate is worth.
  • ✅ Front-load expertise in how you talk to models: short messages, “this looks more complex than I was hoping for” instead of direct contradiction, and you decide the next step. This is not mysticism — it measurably shifts the register of the output.

If you are established (paying customers, real usage):

  • ✅ Pin Akinmade’s line — “if your product requires an FDE, I don’t want it” — above your monitor, then audit your own product for how many parts only you can understand. Every one is a renewal risk and a competitor’s entry point.
  • ✅ Run your eval suite against the cheap models your customers actually use, not the flagship on your subscription. Production failures surface on the small models first.
  • ✅ Inventory every maintainer account and enforce hardware keys. The entry point for 131 poisoned Mastra packages was one employee, one LinkedIn approach, one link.
  • ✅ Write the incident postmortem template now. When an agent does something unexpected inside a customer’s account, the speed and quality of your explanation decides whether you keep them.

What It Means

Two companies made the same bet on the same day from opposite directions. Palantir sells deployment as people and just posted the best quarter in its history. June sells deployment as product and raised $20M from Benioff, Dell, and Levie without opening a slide deck. Neither is selling intelligence. Both are selling the removal of the obstacles between intelligence and a working business process.

That gap does not close when models improve, because it is not made of intelligence. It is made of duplicate fields nobody documented, workflows nobody mapped, permissions nobody audited, and dependencies nobody verified. Every item on that list is knowledge about a specific mess, held by someone who has been inside it.

You cannot outspend anyone in this market. You can know one industry’s mess better than anyone who would bother to compete for it, and you can be the person whose tool does not require a black box to operate.

Pick the mess you already know. Map it before you automate it. Then lock the pipeline you automate it with.
