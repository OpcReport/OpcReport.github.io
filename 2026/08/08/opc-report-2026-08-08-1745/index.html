Cheap Models Caught Up. Now Only Taste and Provenance Are Worth Anything

Cheap Models Caught Up. Now Only Taste and Provenance Are Worth Anything

August 8, 2026 · Issue #13

Key Insights

Two numbers landed within a day of each other this week, and together they break an assumption most of us have been quietly building on.

DeepSeek V4 Flash 0731 scores 61.4% on ARC-AGI-2 at $0.04 per task, and 89.0% on ARC-AGI-1 at $0.02. Meanwhile Databricks published its internal cost playbook and disclosed something more interesting than the playbook itself: Stripe evaluated Opus 4.7, found it did not meaningfully improve quality over 4.6 while costing more, and declined to make it available internally. Databricks saw the same shape comparing Opus 5.0 to 4.8 — a cost regression.

So: the expensive new model is no longer automatically better, and the good-enough cheap model is now cheap enough that everyone has it.

That is this issue’s thesis, and the consequence is uncomfortable. “I ship fast because I use AI well” had a shelf life, and it just expired. When the same tier of intelligence is available to everyone at four cents a task, execution speed stops being a differentiator. Two things remain: knowing what is worth building — the thing a 666-point HN essay this week called taste — and being able to prove where your code came from, which Oracle just turned into an admission requirement for OpenJDK.

Numbers that actually change decisions

  • $0.04 per task for 61.4% on ARC-AGI-2. This is the price anchor for good-enough intelligence. Any product whose cost model assumes frontier pricing should be re-run against this number today.
  • ~300B total parameters, 13B active. Community testing on 2× RTX Pro 6000 reports ~8k tok/s prefill, ~250 tok/s single-stream, ~1000 tok/s at 64 concurrent streams on vLLM. Good-enough intelligence is now local-deployable, which means your unit costs can leave the API bill entirely.
  • Stripe declined Opus 4.7. Databricks logged a cost regression on Opus 5.0 vs 4.8. Upgrading to the newest model is a decision with negative expected value unless you test it yourself.
  • DeepSeek has pre-announced a “significant” API price increase and told users to plan usage accordingly. Today’s pricing is a promotion, not a structural floor.
  • Oracle: $70B in datacenter spend this year, S&P rating cut to BBB-, one notch above junk. Context for why the same company banning AI code from OpenJDK is doing risk accounting, not technical purism.

Market Trends

1. The efficiency frontier is moving faster than the intelligence frontier

The most useful thing in the Databricks post is a distinction, not a technique.

Colloquially, frontier model means the smartest model, and the labs optimize for that. But once AI is deployed at any scale, a different line governs your economics: the efficiency frontier — the set of models with the best price for a given level of intelligence. Their observation is that this line “is advancing far faster than the intelligence frontier, with new models being released almost weekly that present better intelligence-per-unit-price than prior models.”

Most day-to-day coding does not require mathematical proofs or novel security insight. So what determines your bill is not how strong the strongest model is. It is how cheap the merely-sufficient model has become. DeepSeek V4 Flash is a marker on that line — the top HN comment on it was simply: “results comparable to gpt 5.6 luna but cheaper promising!”

This produces a counterintuitive operational rule, and Databricks backs it with two named cases:

  • Public benchmarks cannot drive your selection. Their words: public benchmarks “do a poor job of indicating real-world performance on coding tasks.” Which is why Stripe, Coinbase, Uber, and Ramp all built internal evaluations matched to their own development mix.
  • Those evaluations frequently return negative results, and that is the value. Stripe rejected Opus 4.7. Databricks measured a cost regression on Opus 5.0 vs 4.8. Their own benchmark found GLM models highly competitive on price/performance, which is why GLM got rolled out internally.

For a solo developer: a large fraction of your monthly coding-tool spend is buying intelligence your project does not use. The only way to find out how large is to test against your own codebase — and nobody can do that for you. Which is precisely why it is a product (Direction 2).

2. Enterprises industrialized cost control. The small-team layer is empty.

The Databricks post is effectively a public construction diagram for enterprise AI cost management, built on four levers: move to cheaper models, route dynamically, give developers visibility and budget tripwires, and cut token overhead. They open-sourced or freely released the key pieces — a meta-harness (Omnigent) and an AI Gateway (Unity AI Gateway).

The layer most worth copying at small scale is routing, which has already split into three distinct patterns:

Pattern How it works Watch out for
Request-level routing Stateful proxy between harness and models; each request goes to the cheapest model that can answer it Must be cache-aware — a cold cache hit is extremely expensive on large-context agentic workloads. Examples: Cursor Router, OpenRouter AutoRouter, Ramp Router
Task-level routing (meta-harness) Client-side dispatch by task complexity — “rename X to Y” and “explore latency design tradeoffs” should not hit the same tier Requires a complexity classifier that is itself cheap
Escalation / delegation One harness pairs a cheap model with an expensive one; escalate only when the cheap one stalls Escalation logic is where the savings leak back out

Their argument for the meta-harness is worth internalizing: proprietary models are increasingly co-designed with specific harnesses, so the harness becomes de facto lock-in to a model family. Getting comfortable in one tool quietly costs you the ability to migrate to cheaper models.

The HN hostility to the post is also signal. One commenter called it an ad for Omnigent. Another said removing Databricks saved their company over $2M a year. The vendor has an agenda; the levers are still real. You just have to implement them yourself.

3. Provenance became a real constraint: Oracle banned AI code from OpenJDK

Oracle banned AI-generated code from OpenJDK contributions, citing safety, security, and intellectual property risk. Developers may use LLMs privately for debugging and review, but may not submit AI-generated material to repositories, pull requests, or project channels.

The irony writes itself — Larry Ellison recently declared that AI models now write Oracle’s code, and co-CEO Mike Sicilia credited AI tools with letting smaller engineering teams deliver faster. HN did not let that pass.

But the useful layer is underneath the hypocrisy. This is the first institution with real weight to make “who wrote this code” an admission condition. Top comments immediately extended it: one asked whether Linus should reconsider the legal exposure of accepting AI code into the kernel. Another named the cost honestly — “So they will not be accepting most security patches. Fun.”

The financial context explains the caution better than any principle: Oracle is spending $70 billion on datacenter expansion this year, and S&P cut its rating to BBB-, one notch above junk, citing uncertain returns. Under that kind of balance-sheet pressure, an IP-litigation vector in your flagship open-source project is simply not affordable. This is risk accounting.

What it means: open source projects, regulated industries, and any client needing clean IP attribution will start asking a question nobody asked twelve months ago — which parts of this were machine-written, and can you show me? Almost no tooling exists to answer it.

4. When shipping stops being the bar, the filter is taste

A 666-point essay this week made the argument properly, and the argument is economic rather than sentimental:

“For as long as making things was expensive, the expense did quiet work on our behalf. It rationed output. It meant that anything which existed had, at minimum, survived the cost of being made.”

Effort was a filter, invisible until removed. Nobody shipped a thousand mediocre variants of a feature because a thousand variants cost a thousand times as much. The economics enforced a floor. That floor is gone, so what decides which things deserve to exist is no longer cost — it is judgment. The author is careful that taste is not preference: it is “the compressed, wordless verdict you reach faster than you can justify,” the certainty that two of three plausible versions of a function are wrong.

The essay is worth reading. The top comment is worth memorizing, because it converts the idea into a brutal commercial problem:

“The market timed you both with the same stopwatch and it did not see the difference. It cannot see the difference. Taste does not show up in the diff.”

That commenter goes on to ask the real question: if taste guarantees market failure, how does anyone enter the market at all — the market will just clone you.

The most useful reply gave the only realistic exit:

“To adapt to this AI world, we need to either ship more lower-quality software, or start seeking out higher value problems. I don’t see any other way around it.”

Pin that above your idea list. With execution commoditized, problem selection is where nearly all remaining leverage lives.

(A footnote with its own lesson: the author had to append a post-mortem because HN’s front page read the piece as AI slop. The “reads like AI” penalty flagged in the last issue is not softening.)

5. Agents are getting their own infrastructure layer

Cloudflare shipped Kitesurf, a browser built entirely on Workers and designed for agents rather than people. The reasoning matters more than the product:

“AI doesn’t care about tabs, themes, browser extensions, or synchronization across devices. It cares about token count, context windows, scalability, performance, and costs.”

And on quality tradeoffs: structured machine-readable content matters, but “agents will be just fine if the CSS parsing is slightly off or the rendering isn’t pixel perfect.” Their motivating constraint is economic — Chromium instances consume so much memory and compute that giving every agent one is prohibitive, “restricting large parts of the Web to only the most sophisticated and costly AI models.”

One detail deserves attention from anyone building alone: Cloudflare credits its inspiration to obscura, an open-source headless engine written in Rust with “no Chrome, no Node.js, no dependencies.” A solo project became the starting point for a Cloudflare product.

The same week, CopilotKit shipped an MIT-licensed Channels SDK putting agents into Slack and Teams. Same direction: the agent’s runtime environment is separating from the human’s, and every layer of that new environment is currently under-tooled.

Build Directions

Five directions, with stack, cost, and timeline.

Direction 1 — Cost control for individual developers (biggest gap this issue)

What to build: Databricks published the enterprise answer, but it assumes you have a platform team. A solo developer burning a few hundred dollars a month on coding tools needs the same routing, visibility, and budget tripwires, and nothing is built for them. Ship a local proxy that sits between Claude Code / Cursor / Codex and the models: cheap tier by default, automatic escalation on stall, per-task cost logging.

Layer Choice Why
Study first Databricks’ four levers + Unity AI Gateway, Omnigent The design work is public; you are building the small version
Proxy Go or TypeScript, OpenAI/Anthropic-compatible endpoint User changes one base_url — lowest possible adoption friction
Critical detail Cache-aware routing Cold cache hits are extremely expensive on large contexts. A naive router costs more than no router. This is your actual moat
Cheap tier DeepSeek V4 Flash, GLM family, mini/flash tiers Keep it configurable — DeepSeek has pre-announced a price increase
Interface CLI + local dashboard No hosting, no data leaving the machine, no compliance conversation

Cost & timeline: v1 in 3–5 weeks, near-zero infrastructure. Price at $15–39/month against demonstrated savings — the easiest value proposition to prove on this list.

Direction 2 — Codebase-specific model evaluation

What to build: the clearest gap the data points at. Public benchmarks do not predict real coding performance, so Stripe, Uber, Ramp, and Coinbase all built internal evals — a capability currently limited to companies that can staff it. Build a tool that scans a user’s repo and recent commits, generates tasks matching their actual work distribution, runs candidate models across them, and reports which model is cheapest-sufficient for this codebase.

Layer Choice Why
Task generation Mine real diffs from git history as regression tasks Closer to the user’s true distribution than any synthetic benchmark — and the only real moat here
Execution Python + containerized sandbox, SQLite results Local-first removes the “do I upload my code to you” objection entirely
Model access OpenRouter for breadth One integration covers most candidates
Killer output Negative results “Do not upgrade” is the finding that saved Stripe and Databricks real money

Cost & timeline: 4–6 weeks to something credible. $49–99 one-time, or $29/month to track new releases continuously. Buyer is technical and already spending.

Direction 3 — Code provenance and AI contribution disclosure

What to build: Oracle just made “who wrote this” an admission requirement and no convenient tooling exists. A git-layer provenance tool: track which hunks originated in agent sessions versus human authorship or rewrite, generate a submittable contribution disclosure, and offer an automated PR check.

Stack: git hooks + agent session log ingestion → SQLite local index → MCP server interface (free distribution inside Claude Code and Cursor) → optional PR bot.

Beachhead: open source maintainers, not enterprise compliance. They face the decision now, they are reachable on HN, and there is no procurement cycle. Enterprise is step two.

Honest risk: this rests on more institutions following Oracle. Right now it is a single data point. Build the light version as a side bet, not your main line.

Cost & timeline: 3–6 weeks. Open core + $10–29/user/month for teams.

Direction 4 — Data interfaces built for agents, not people

What to build: Kitesurf’s existence is the argument — agent web access is expensive enough to justify rebuilding the browser. Inverted: anything that delivers data in a shape agents can consume directly is selling that cost saving. Pick one vertical data source you know, ship it as a clean structured API plus MCP server, charge per call.

Layer Choice Why
Extraction Lightweight headless engine (see obscura’s approach) Full Chromium destroys your unit economics from day one
Delivery REST + MCP MCP is the shortest current path to agent users
Hosting Cloudflare Workers or similar edge Cold starts and costs a solo project can absorb
Selection rule Ugly page structure + decision-relevant data The worse the HTML, the more your middle layer is worth. Government data, industry directories, vertical price lists

Cost & timeline: 2–4 weeks for a single source. Usage-based, $0.001–0.01/call to start.

Direction 5 — Local-first single-purpose tools competing on taste

What to build: the only actionable response to the taste essay. Features get cloned and the market cannot see taste in a diff — so pick something small enough that no large company will bother, that you personally redo by hand every week, and solve it completely. Fully local, one-time or commercial license.

Layer Choice Why
Language Rust or Go Single binary; install is one download — the install experience is part of the taste
Processing Fully local Kills hosting cost, latency, and compliance objections at once
Interface CLI + MCP Callable from coding agents for free
Ideation Read your own shell history from the last month Find the thing you redo weekly. That is the entire process — do not brainstorm

Cost & timeline: 2–5 weeks, near-zero running cost. $29–79 flat, commercial license separate.

Risk Factors

  • Today’s pricing is a promotion, not a floor. DeepSeek has pre-announced a significant API price increase and told users to plan accordingly. Any margin built on one cheap provider is renting someone else’s pricing strategy. Build model-swappable from day one.
  • Benchmark scores are not agentic reliability. Developers report this V4 Flash release getting stuck in self-talk loops without executing tool calls, burning tokens, and drifting to unrelated topics while unable to notice it when pointed out. Run your own long-horizon agent tasks before you ship on a cheap model.
  • “Fast because AI” is no longer differentiation. The moment good-enough intelligence is universally affordable, speed advantage goes to zero. The market timed you both with the same stopwatch. Problem selection now dominates execution speed.
  • Agent-written code can be a liability in genuinely complex systems. A well-received HN argument: at 500k lines with >50% agent-authored, long-term maintenance pain outweighs short-term velocity; agents suit projects with many features that do not interact. Be honest about which kind you are building before committing to a vertical SaaS.
  • Hardware costs are moving the wrong way. 2027 memory capacity is reportedly sold out. The local-deployment escape route is not getting cheaper soon — re-run the “buy two cards and save” math.
  • Institutional attitudes to AI code can shift fast. Oracle’s ban is isolated today. If kernel-scale projects follow, “fully AI-built” turns from a marketing line into something you have to explain. Start retaining development provenance now — it is cheap today and may be valuable later.

Action Recommendations

If you are early (no stable product yet)

  • ✅ Run a tier-downgrade test this week. Take three real recent tasks, run each on your current model and on a cheap tier, log cost and rework. Most people find half their tasks never needed the expensive model. This is simultaneously a cost saving and the product research for Directions 1 and 2.
  • ✅ Switch your selection criterion from “what can I build” to “what is worth building.” Buildability is no longer the constraint. Either ship more low-quality software or go find higher-value problems.
  • ✅ Start with Direction 1 or 5. Shortest timelines (2–5 weeks), lowest cost, and neither depends on an unproven trend. Direction 3 carries the most assumption risk — do not make it your first project.
  • ✅ Mine your own shell history for the idea. The thing you redo by hand every week is the only product lead where you have an information advantage.

If you already have product and users

  • ✅ Compute inference cost as a share of revenue, then walk the four levers. Cheaper models, routing, visibility, token overhead. All four are pure margin improvement with no product change.
  • ✅ Stop auto-upgrading to the newest model. Stripe and Databricks both measured regressions. Build a regression set of even ten real tasks and run it on every model change — highest-ROI engineering hour on this list.
  • ✅ Add an MCP interface. Near-zero marginal cost, and it places your tool inside Claude Code and Cursor where the work happens.
  • ✅ Audit your provider lock-in. If switching models takes more than a day of code changes, you are locked in. Build the abstraction before the price increase lands, not after.
  • ✅ Start logging AI usage provenance. Not for compliance — so you have an answer when a client asks. Cheap now, expensive to reconstruct later.

What It Means

For two years the implicit strategy for a solo builder was straightforward: adopt the best model early, ship faster than people who had not, and let velocity substitute for headcount. That worked because good models were expensive and using them well was a skill.

Both halves of that just weakened. Sufficient intelligence costs four cents a task and runs on hardware you can buy. The most expensive model is no longer reliably the best one — two serious engineering organizations measured that and declined the upgrade. When everyone has the same tool at the same price, the tool is not the advantage.

What is left is unglamorous and hard to fake. Choosing a problem worth solving, which no model does for you and which the market rewards long before it notices your craftsmanship. And being able to account for what you shipped — a question that did not exist a year ago and that Oracle just made a condition of contribution.

The practical read: spend this week measuring rather than building. Find out what fraction of your model spend buys nothing, and what fraction of your roadmap is a problem worth having. The cheapest move on this list — testing three real tasks against a cheap tier — pays for itself immediately and tells you whether Direction 1 or 2 is a business. Start there.
